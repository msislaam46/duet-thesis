\chapter{Legal, Social, Ethical and Professional Issues}\label{ch:lsepi}

This project develops a security research prototype that integrates with open-source Signal code. Its
subject matter and integration surface raise legal, social, ethical, and professional considerations.
This chapter examines how those considerations informed the work. It considers the legal position of the code and the data
(\cref{sec:lsepi-legal}), the social and ethical implications of strengthening, and of being
seen to strengthen, end-to-end-encrypted messaging (\cref{sec:lsepi-social}), and the
professional standards, specifically the BCS Code of Conduct~\cite{bcs-code} and the IET Rules of
Conduct~\cite{iet-conduct}, against which the work was conducted (\cref{sec:lsepi-prof}).

\section{Legal Issues}\label{sec:lsepi-legal}

Intellectual property and licensing are the most immediate legal consideration. The prototype
extends two Signal codebases, libsignal and Signal Desktop, both licensed under the GNU
Affero General Public License v3 (AGPL-3.0)~\cite{libsignal,signal-desktop}. Conveying modified
covered code would engage the licence's corresponding-source obligations. If a modified program
supporting network interaction were offered as a remote service, AGPL~\S13 would additionally require
its users to be offered the corresponding source. The prototype was not operated as a public network
service, and the upstream copyright and licence notices are preserved throughout. Whether a
particular separately developed component forms part of a combined or derivative work depends on the
facts, the jurisdiction, and the mode of combination and distribution, and is not determined here.
The independent Rust \texttt{duet} crate depends on permissively licensed libraries, including
\texttt{sha2} and \texttt{hkdf} (MIT/Apache-2.0) and \texttt{curve25519-dalek} and
\texttt{ed25519-dalek} (BSD-3-Clause), which are compatible with those obligations. All third-party
work (specifications, prior systems, and code) is attributed in the references.

A second consideration is export control. Cryptographic software may fall within UK strategic export
controls depending on its functionality, destination, end use, and the applicable control-list
provisions. Public source availability and the use of published algorithms do not alone determine
classification. The contribution composes standardized primitives (the RFC~9381 ECVRF, RFC~5869 HKDF,
and Ed25519) without introducing a new cryptographic primitive. This thesis therefore makes no
export-control determination for DUET or its dependencies.

Data protection is the third. Key transparency is, by construction, a data-minimizing design: the
directory stores public keys and pseudonymous labels, never message content, and the VRF-derived
global labels (\cref{ssec:design-labels}) deliberately prevent an observer from enumerating which
users are present. The benchmark corpus used synthetic identifiers and contained no
intended references to real individuals, so the measurements did not process personal data. A
production directory would nevertheless process public keys and labels that the service operator may
be able to associate with accounts. Such pseudonymous information may remain personal data under
UK GDPR, so a deployment would require a separate data-protection assessment, access controls,
retention limits, and data-minimized operational logging of warnings and audit events, so that
the alerting layer does not re-expose the label-to-user metadata the pseudorandom labels are
designed to hide. Screenshots and operational logs that contain identifiable names or paths would
require separate review before release.

\section{Social and Ethical Issues}\label{sec:lsepi-social}

The social value of the work is a direct contribution to public wellbeing and security:
authenticated key exchange is what makes end-to-end encryption meaningful, and the people who most
depend on it (journalists, activists, and others at risk) are exactly those whom an
undetected key substitution would endanger. Strengthening the automatic detection of such attacks,
and giving both conversation partners an in-band warning, serves the public interest that the BCS
code places first.

That same importance imposes a duty of honesty about what the work does and does not achieve,
because overstating a security guarantee can expose users to risks the system does not address. The thesis is explicit
about its limitations: the prototype carries two proof-of-concept stand-ins for live protocol state
(\cref{sec:impl-strategy}), it is a single-machine prototype rather than a distributed deployment,
and the boundary case of key extraction is out of scope (\cref{ch:threat}). The user-facing design
embodies the same caution: an unreachable server produces an amber availability warning, while red is
reserved for integrity-related conditions such as a proof failure, key mismatch, or authenticated
peer warning (\cref{ssec:impl-verifier}), so that frequent availability
warnings do not undermine confidence in integrity alerts. The directly implemented ECVRF is checked against the
RFC~9381 vectors, and reproducible tests exercise the remaining protocol and integration paths
(\cref{sec:impl-tests}).

A dual-use dimension is unavoidable: strong messaging security protects everyone, including those
who would misuse it. This project treats defensive integrity infrastructure of this kind as serving the public good,
since weakening it to disadvantage bad actors would also weaken protection for the far larger
population of ordinary and at-risk users; the professional codes cited in \cref{sec:lsepi-prof}
support this position. Finally, the work followed responsible-disclosure practice. Review of the
prototype identified and corrected several security-relevant defects, including a gap in binding each
lookup to its signed tree head (\cref{sec:impl-challenges}). These findings concerned DUET itself; no
vulnerability in deployed Signal software was identified, and any such vulnerability would require
private disclosure to Signal before publication.

A warning mechanism also has direct effects on the people who see it, which a responsible design must
weigh. Because the channel can be written by a peer, a malicious or compromised contact can raise a
spurious warning; at scale, frequent or unexplained alerts risk \emph{warning fatigue}, in which users
learn to dismiss the very signal meant to protect them (\cref{sec:disc-limitations}). The design
mitigates this by distinguishing an integrity-related alert (red) from mere unavailability (amber) and
by deriving the warning from cryptographic evidence rather than heuristics. Red flags an integrity
condition, such as a proof failure, key mismatch, or authenticated peer warning, without by itself
identifying the cause or proving that the directory operator attacked the conversation. The wording,
frequency, and explicability of the alert are a human-factors problem this work does not claim to have
solved, and getting them wrong could erode trust rather than build it. The stakes are highest for at-risk users:
in an intimate-partner-abuse setting a key change, or a warning about one, can carry safety
implications beyond the cryptographic question, and any deployment should treat the accessibility and
clear explanation of these alerts, and a safe path to seek help, as first-order requirements. On the
conduct of the research itself, finally, all adversarial functionality was exercised only in an
isolated local test harness against synthetic accounts, with no live Signal interception and no real
users (\cref{ssec:impl-demo}).

\section{Professional Issues and Codes of Conduct}\label{sec:lsepi-prof}

The work was carried out in line with the BCS Code of Conduct~\cite{bcs-code} and the IET Rules of
Conduct~\cite{iet-conduct}. The following paragraphs relate the implementation and evaluation
practices to the relevant provisions of both codes.

The BCS code is organized under four principles. \emph{Public interest} (due regard for
security, privacy, and wellbeing) is the project's subject matter, addressed above.
\emph{Professional competence and integrity} shaped the technical choices: established
cryptographic libraries were used in preference to bespoke implementations, and the one primitive
implemented directly, the ECVRF, was tested against the RFC~9381 vectors
(\cref{ssec:impl-core-crypto}); claims are held to what the evidence supports. \emph{Duty to the
relevant authority} was met through honest, regular engagement with the project supervisor and
adherence to the university's academic-integrity requirements, including full attribution of
sources and a clear demarcation of the proof-of-concept stand-ins from verified results.
\emph{Duty to the profession} is served by not overstating the contribution and by documenting the
design and its limitations for scrutiny.

The IET Rules of Conduct, which adopt the Engineering Council and Royal Academy of Engineering
Statement of Ethical Principles (Rule~2), impose corresponding obligations. Their emphasis on \emph{accuracy and
rigour} is reflected in the bounded and invariant-oriented testing and the reproducibility measures
(\cref{sec:impl-tests,sec:impl-repro}); their requirement to work within one's competence and to
keep knowledge current (Rules~5--6) is reflected in grounding standard components in named
specifications and documenting project-specific mechanisms separately, and their insistence that a professional opinion state its limitations clearly
(Rule~26) is reflected in the explicit scoping of the claims throughout. Above all, both codes
place the public good first. Protecting the public is therefore a central professional objective of
this project, which aims to make a widely used private-messaging system harder to attack.
