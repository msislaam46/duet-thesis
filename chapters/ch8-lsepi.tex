\chapter{Legal, Social, Ethical and Professional Issues}\label{ch:lsepi}

Integrating a security research prototype with open-source Signal code raises questions of licensing,
data protection, user impact, and professional responsibility.

% ============================================================================
\section{Legal Issues}\label{sec:lsepi-legal}

The prototype extends two Signal codebases, libsignal and Signal Desktop, both licensed under the GNU
Affero General Public License v3 (AGPL-3.0)~\cite{libsignal,signal-desktop}. Conveying modified
covered code would engage the licence's corresponding-source obligations. If a modified program
supporting network interaction were offered as a remote service, AGPL~\S13 would additionally require
its users to be offered the corresponding source~\cite{agpl3}. The prototype was not operated as a
public network service, and the upstream copyright and licence notices are preserved throughout.
Whether a particular separately developed component forms part of a combined or derivative work
depends on the facts, the jurisdiction, and the mode of combination and distribution, and is not
determined here.

The Signal Foundation could evaluate the work through Signal Messenger's software projects, either by
independently implementing the published design~\cite{uk-software-copyright} or by reviewing an
upstream code contribution~\cite{signal-foundation-structure}. Reusing the Rust implementation would
require confirmation of ownership and an explicit licence. Changes contributed to Signal Desktop or
libsignal would remain subject to AGPL-3.0 and Signal's contributor agreement~\cite{signal-cla}. Any
adoption would also require Signal's own security, privacy, export-control, and deployment review.
These are possible routes rather than a legal determination.

Cryptographic software may be subject to UK export controls depending on its functionality,
destination, and end use~\cite{uk-cryptonote}. Public specifications and open-source libraries do not
by themselves determine classification. No export-control classification is attempted here.

DUET's directory stores public keys and pseudonymous labels rather than message content. The
benchmark inputs were synthetic and were not linked to real people. In production, the operator may
associate keys and labels with accounts. Such pseudonymised information remains
personal data for an operator holding the linking information~\cite{ico-pseudonymisation}. Production
use would require a data-protection assessment, restricted access to warning records, and defined
retention periods.

\section{Social and Ethical Issues}\label{sec:lsepi-social}

Undetected key substitution is particularly serious for journalists, activists, and other users who
rely on confidential messaging. Automatic checks and in-band peer warnings reduce dependence on
manual verification and support the public-interest duties discussed in \cref{sec:lsepi-prof}.

That same importance imposes a duty of honesty about what the work does and does not achieve, because
overstating a security guarantee can expose users to risks the system does not address. The thesis is
explicit about its limitations: the prototype carries two proof-of-concept stand-ins for live
protocol state (\cref{sec:impl-strategy}), it is a single-machine prototype rather than a distributed
deployment, and the boundary case of key extraction is out of scope (\cref{ch:threat}).

Stronger messaging security can also be used by malicious actors, but weakening authentication would
expose all users to silent key substitution. Review of DUET identified and corrected several
security-relevant defects, including missing signed-head binding (\cref{sec:impl-challenges}). These
findings concerned the prototype rather than deployed Signal software. Any future vulnerability
affecting deployed Signal code should be disclosed privately before publication.

Warnings can themselves cause harm when they are frequent, unexplained, or raised by an abusive peer.
The interface therefore distinguishes unavailable checks from integrity-related conditions and
authenticated peer warnings. A red warning does not identify the cause or prove an operator attack.
Production deployment would require usability testing of the wording, accessibility, escalation
process, and support paths for at-risk users. All adversarial functionality was exercised only in an
isolated local test harness against synthetic accounts, with no live Signal interception and no real
users (\cref{ssec:impl-demo}).

\section{Professional Issues and Codes of Conduct}\label{sec:lsepi-prof}

The BCS Code of Conduct identifies four areas of professional responsibility~\cite{bcs-code}. Under
the public-interest principle, the thesis considers security, privacy, warning-related harm, and users
who depend on confidential messaging. Consistent with professional competence and integrity, the
implementation uses established cryptographic libraries where available and tests the ECVRF
implementation against the RFC~9381 vectors. Regular supervisory review, source attribution, and
compliance with the university's academic-integrity requirements reflect the duty to the relevant
authority. Separating measured prototype results from unimplemented production requirements reflects
the duty to the profession.

The IET Rules of Conduct impose related obligations~\cite{iet-conduct}. Rule~2 incorporates the
Engineering Council and Royal Academy of Engineering Statement of Ethical Principles, including
accuracy and rigour. Rules~5 and~6 require members to maintain their knowledge and undertake only work
they are competent to perform. Rule~26 requires professional opinions to state their limitations and
qualifications. The adversarial tests, cross-language vectors, reproducibility measures, and explicit
scope qualifications reported in \cref{ch:impl,ch:eval,ch:discussion} demonstrate how these obligations
were applied in the project.
