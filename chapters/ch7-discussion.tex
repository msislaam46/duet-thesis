\chapter{Discussion, Limitations and Future Work}\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter steps back from the construction and the numbers to ask what they mean. It first
discusses what the results establish and how the design sits within the wider key-transparency
literature (\cref{sec:disc-discussion}), then states the limitations of the work candidly
(\cref{sec:disc-limitations}), and finally sets out the future work the design invites: the part
that would be needed to turn the prototype into a deployable system (\cref{sec:disc-future}).

% ============================================================================
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc-discussion}

This section interprets the results of \cref{ch:eval}: what the measurements establish, where the
detection guarantee actually comes from, how far the design generalizes beyond Signal, and the
deployments for which it is the wrong tool.

\paragraph{Interpretation: Detection at No Asymptotic Cost.} The central finding of this work is that
automatic, in-band detection of key substitution can be
added to a messaging directory without changing the asymptotic cost of the proofs the directory
already serves, at the measured cost of additional proof-shaped lookups. \Cref{ch:eval} shows that the implemented VRF-indexed lookup
proof is at rough size parity with CONIKS while carrying a warning channel CONIKS lacks, that full client
median verification stays below \SI{150}{\micro\second} through $10^7$ labels and grows far more
slowly than $N$ (\SIrange{128.1}{147.1}{\micro\second}), and that an offline
client catches up in a few hundred bytes where naïve monitoring would spend hundreds of kilobytes.
The detection the design adds does not show up as a new asymptotic cost: it rides on the same
logarithmic proofs the directory already produces. This result supports the deployment
argument, because it means a provider does not have to choose between the transparency it already
runs and the peer-warning channel that closes the gap manual safety numbers leave open.

\paragraph{Where the Guarantee Comes From.} How the detection actually
holds deserves care, because the substitution surface is larger than it first appears. A malicious server has two slots in which it can plant a victim's key:
the global VRF-derived slot, which it can both compute and overwrite, and the per-conversation
dual-index slot, which it cannot derive without the shared secret but can overwrite through the
registration-metadata link. The scheme does not prevent either overwrite: a server can always
rewrite its own database. Detection instead rests on two facts established in \cref{ch:design}. The
warning slot is derived by an independent HKDF label, so knowledge of a lookup label yields no
ability to compute the warning label, and once a warning is committed the server cannot hide it
silently: refusing to serve it is a total, observable write outage (proven non-inclusion turns
censorship into a visible denial of service), and removing it in a later epoch is a deletion the
declared update set cannot express, so the transition audit catches the divergent replay and the
writer's persistence check converts the disappearance into publishable evidence
(\cref{ssec:design-detection}). And under an active network man-in-the-middle, the two parties derive their
labels from divergent shared secrets; the attacker, lacking the true secret, cannot predict the
victim's expected warning label and so faces an empty slot it cannot fill with a valid inclusion
proof. Detection is therefore a property of where the keys must live, not of the server's good
behaviour.

\paragraph{Generalizability Beyond Signal.} Nothing in the design is specific to Signal beyond the
shape of its inputs: it applies to any messenger with a semi-trusted key directory and a per-session
shared secret from a ratcheting handshake, from which the labels are derived. In that light, the work
also instantiates and measures this piece of the ACKA framework (the instantiation claimed, with
its survey scope, in \cref{sec:bg-contrib}) rather than correcting it. ACKA~\cite{dowling2023acka} deliberately leaves its
append-only-log component abstract; that abstraction is a framework-design choice, not a flaw, and
this thesis fills it in with a Merkle log, sparse prefix tree, and ECVRF following the IETF KeyTrans
draft~\cite{ietf-keytrans-protocol}, then measures what that instantiation costs
(\cref{sec:design-acka}). The dual-index warning is best described as an \emph{extension} of the
continuous-authentication idea rather than an instance of it: where the framework's fingerprints are
values chained from ratchet state, the dual-index scheme derives \emph{labels}, locations in the
directory, and uses a shared one as a warning slot. Stating this precisely matters, because the
contribution is the shared-warning-slot mechanism and its cost, not a re-derivation of the framework.

\paragraph{Deployment Trade-Offs, and Where Not to Use the Design.} The design provides detection
rather than prevention. A server can still
substitute a key; what changes is that the substitution can no longer be silent. That framing
inherits the trust-on-first-use caveat of the underlying protocol, the very first key a party
learns is taken on faith, and it deliberately leaves several things out of scope: it does not
provide endpoint security, it does not hide the directory's contents from the operator, and it
addresses pairwise conversations rather than groups. What it offers a deployment such as Signal is a
narrow, well-defined addition: a write path for warning and per-session labels alongside the existing
identity directory, at a client-side cost of microseconds per conversation. By the same token, it is
the wrong tool where the threat is a compromised endpoint rather than a dishonest directory (nothing
is protected once the adversary holds the user's own key), and its equivocation guarantee is only as
strong as the availability of an independent second vantage: where no honest auditor or gossip channel
exists, fork detection (Goal~G4) should not be relied on, though dual-index substitution detection
still holds without one.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The honest limits of the work fall into four groups: what the design is scoped to, what it must trust,
what the prototype does not yet realize, and what has not been proved.

\paragraph{Scope.} The scheme is detection, not prevention, and it inherits the trust-on-first-use
assumption discussed above. It is pairwise: it does not cover group messaging, Sender Keys, or MLS,
where the warning would have to fan out across a dynamic membership. And it is single-device, in
contrast to multi-device keychain schemes such as ELEKTRA~\cite{len2023elektra}; extending the
warning to a set of a user's devices is left open.

\paragraph{Trust Assumptions.} Two of the design's guarantees rest on parties beyond the client. Fork
detection requires at least one honest auditor with an independent vantage point; this is scoped
precisely (dual-index detection needs no auditor, and only split-view equivocation does) and it
is implemented as a running cross-vantage check (\cref{ssec:eval-auditor}). In the prototype the
second vantage is a server-served stand-in driven by the testing interface; a production deployment
would feed that second head over a real gossip channel or an independent auditor, with identical
detection logic and only the transport changed. Separately, the STRIDE analysis
(\cref{ssec:eval-stride}) leaves four residual threats, none a weakness in this construction. One is
inherent to the semi-trusted-server model and shared by every deployed key-transparency system: the
operator necessarily learns the label-to-user mapping (there is no query privacy from the operator).
The other three are availability functions the proof-of-concept does not implement (write
rate-limiting, a published-epoch staleness timeout, and a gossip transport for proof-omission), each
a deployment-layer engineering task rather than a gap in the scheme.

\paragraph{Prototype Bounds.} Several gaps separate the measured prototype from a deployable one. The
benchmarks are single-machine and in-memory, with no networked or database-backed deployment, so they
characterize the design's algorithmic cost rather than a production service; relatedly, the epoch
commit uses an $O(N)$ deep-clone snapshot with a transient memory spike, measured directly in
\cref{ch:eval} and reducible to $O(\log N)$ by structural sharing (below). The Signal Desktop
integration carries two proof-of-concept stand-ins, both flagged in \cref{ch:impl}: the
per-conversation shared secret is a deterministic stand-in rather than the live X3DH secret, and
continuous mode is driven by a seeded simulated ratchet rather than the client's live one. That
simulation, specifically, folds the raw ratchet Diffie--Hellman output as its epoch secret $I_t$,
where ACKA's Signal mapping (their Table~2) specifies $I_t = ck$, the derived chain key; so DUET
implements the ACKA CEA state transition but simulates that Signal-specific epoch-secret mapping, and
a faithful instantiation bridges the live Double-Ratchet chain key $ck$ from libsignal. Neither
changes the construction being exercised, but both must be replaced before the unlinkability and
active-MitM guarantees hold against a real adversary rather than a simulated one. Finally, the
networked client ties every search-time signed tree head into one append-only chain: the head is
signature-verified per lookup and its root is folded, via the same windowed consistency proof used
for offline catch-up, into the client's pinned baseline, so a validly-signed but \emph{forked} head
served only in a search response (and thus invisible to an auditor polling the public head alone) is
rejected the instant it fails to chain. The residual here is not a client-side gap but the fundamental
limitation already stated above: a \emph{fully consistent} split-world, whose branches each chain from
a shared prefix, cannot be detected by any lone client and relies on the cross-vantage auditor.

\paragraph{Formal Status.} The security argument is an informal proof sketch supported by explicit experiment
definitions and the adversarial scenario suite; it is not a complete cryptographic reduction. A combined
``dual-CEAsec'' game and its reduction to collision resistance and VRF pseudorandomness are stated as
an obligation rather than discharged. A further practical limitation is warning-channel abuse: a
malicious peer can write spurious warnings, a relationship-scoped false-positive nuisance. The
implemented payload MAC (\cref{ch:design}) authenticates the operator boundary (a reader can
distinguish a peer-authored warning from operator-injected noise) but cannot help here, because
an abusive peer holds the shared secret and can mint valid MACs; a production system would blunt
this with rate limiting and per-sender signed warning payloads.

\paragraph{Evaluation Completeness.} Three further gaps bound what the evaluation establishes, and
each is a matter of proof-of-concept scope rather than a hidden result. First, the adversarial suite
(\cref{app:tests}) models substitution against an \emph{already-established} conversation, the
scheme's stated scope (\cref{ssec:design-detection}), and a network man-in-the-middle against an
honest global directory; it does not model a two-leg adversary interposed at first contact, nor a
server that makes a fabricated pre-key bundle and its log entry mutually consistent, both of which lie
outside the post-establishment guarantee. Second, the principal latency numbers measure components,
not a live end-to-end exchange: M11 is the KT lookup-verification cost at the handshake hook (identical to M3), not
a Signal/PQXDH handshake benchmark, and M12 is the auditor's local computation, not the networked
proof-carrying audit. Third, the DUET test suite is not wired into Signal Desktop's main continuous
integration and the live wire-parity check skips silently when no Rust server is reachable, and only
aggregate benchmark statistics, not raw Criterion samples, are committed. A two-leg adversarial
harness, a genuine handshake benchmark, a required CI gate, and archived raw samples for independent
re-analysis are left to future work.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:disc-future}

The limitations above map onto a concrete programme of future work. The items are ordered roughly from
those that turn the prototype into a deployment to those that broaden the design's reach.

\paragraph{Production Shared-Secret Binding.} The single change that most advances the work is
extracting the genuine X3DH/PQXDH shared secret from libsignal at the pre-key-bundle hook and binding
the dual-index labels to it, in place of the deterministic stand-in. libsignal folds the shared
secret into the ratchet root key and discards it immediately after session initialization, so the
work is to capture it at that hook and hand it to the label derivation; everything downstream is
unchanged. This is what turns the labels from ``stable per account'' into ``unguessable by the server,
per session,'' and it is the prerequisite for the active-network-MitM detection of \cref{ch:threat} to
hold against a real adversary. It is the principal deployment-path item.

\paragraph{An $O(\log N)$ Epoch Commit.} The commit cost measured in \cref{ch:eval} is an artefact of
a single-process design that deep-clones the committed tree so concurrent lookups see a stable view.
Replacing the tree's owned children with reference-counted, copy-on-write nodes makes each epoch an
immutable version that shares all untouched subtrees with its predecessor and allocates only along the
$O(\log N)$ root-to-leaf path, matching how production key-transparency systems version epochs. It is a
self-contained server-side change, orthogonal to the verification-side contribution.

\paragraph{Authenticated Ownership of Raw-Label Writes.} \Cref{ch:design} specifies three safeguards that together guarantee a committed warning or
CEA fingerprint cannot be silently removed or replaced: a write-once policy on dual-index and CEA
labels, a per-epoch transition audit in which the auditor replays each epoch's disclosed update set
against the previous committed root (the role the IETF KeyTrans protocol assigns to third-party
auditors through its \textsf{AuditorUpdate} structure~\cite{ietf-keytrans-protocol}), and a
writer-side persistence check that retains inclusion proofs and converts any later disappearance of
a committed leaf into non-repudiable, publishable evidence of deletion. The prototype now implements
all three: the server's raw-label update interface enforces the write-once policy and rejects an
overwrite of an occupied dual-index, CEA, or warning label; each committed epoch records a declared
update set that the auditor replays onto its own mirror directory, checking the result against the
committed root, so a deletion, which the delta format cannot express, makes the replay diverge
and is detected; and the writer-side persistence check retains the inclusion proof and signed tree
head of a committed warning and, on any later disappearance, assembles the inclusion proof, the
proven non-inclusion, and the consistency proof between the two heads into a bundle that verifies
against the server's own signatures. The one remaining deployment-path item is authenticated writes
(account-layer proof that a caller owns the raw label it writes), without which the write-once
policy still binds an honest server and the auditor still binds a malicious one, but a malicious
\emph{client} could squat an as-yet-empty peer slot. The in-process implementation and its
adversarial tests are described in \cref{ch:impl}; the argument that these safeguards bind
a fully malicious operator is exactly the suppression argument of \cref{ch:design}.

\paragraph{A Formal Dual-CEAsec Reduction.} The narrative security argument can be completed into a
game-based proof: define the combined detection game over the dual-index scheme and the log, and
reduce an adversary's advantage to the collision resistance of the hash and the pseudorandomness and
uniqueness of the VRF, discharging the obligations sketched in \cref{ch:design}. A complementary
step is machine-checked assurance: a symbolic model of the detection protocol in a prover such as
Tamarin or ProVerif and, further out, a verified implementation of the client-side proof checkers.

\paragraph{Group Messaging.} Extending the scheme to groups is the largest conceptual step. Index
derivation would draw on the group's shared secrets, for example TreeKEM secrets under MLS, and
the warning mechanism would need a fan-out strategy across a membership that changes over time, along
with a treatment of how joins and removals interact with the warning slot.

\paragraph{Query Privacy via a Distributed OPRF.} A privacy property not provided by the deployed
systems surveyed here, hiding the queried identity from the operator, is a natural extension. A client could blind its
lookup identity with an oblivious pseudorandom function (RFC~9497~\cite{rfc9497}) before the server
applies its VRF, so the operator learns neither who is queried nor by whom. Signal has shipped a
distributed OPRF over Ristretto255 in its SVR3 secure-value-recovery scheme~\cite{libsignal},
since retired in favour of enclave-based recovery, so the engineering pattern is proven in
Signal's own lineage; adapting it to the label derivation is the open work.

\paragraph{Post-Quantum, Multi-Device, and Deployment.} Because the labels are hash-derived, a
post-quantum shared secret (PQXDH~\cite{pqxdh}) can feed the derivation unchanged; the open piece is a
post-quantum VRF, for which only candidates exist. Multi-device support would follow the keychain
cross-signing of ELEKTRA~\cite{len2023elektra}. On the deployment path, the cleanest route is to
integrate with Signal's existing key-transparency service~\cite{signal-kt-server} behind a feature
flag rather than running a parallel log, which requires only a write API for the warning and
per-session labels, and the fork-detection vantage should be supplied by a real auditor gossip
ecosystem rather than the demo stand-in, which raises its own open questions of federation
across providers and of incentives for independent auditors to participate honestly.

\paragraph{Human-Centred Warning Design.} The evaluation shows that the warning fires; it does not
show that users act on it. A usability study of the warning surfaces (how users interpret the
red and amber verdicts, whether the evidence behind an alert can be explained intelligibly, how
the warnings meet accessibility requirements, and how alarm fatigue is avoided) would complete
the chain from cryptographic detection to human response, continuing the line of ceremony studies
that motivated the design~\cite{vaziripour2017}.

\paragraph{Evidence-Carrying Alerts and Operational Response.} The prototype's verdicts are
user-facing events. A production service would add an operator-facing layer with three parts. First,
a structured alert schema carrying the scenario class, the epoch, and the signed tree heads and proofs
that constitute the evidence. Second, notification interfaces through which an operator or auditor can
reproduce an incident from that evidence alone. Third, an explicit response policy defining when the
system rejects a proof, requests a key rotation, or escalates to a human. Together these give
automated detection with human-in-the-loop response. Any such telemetry must be minimized so that the alerting layer does
not itself leak the metadata the pseudorandom labels were designed to hide.

\paragraph{A Networked, Persistent Benchmark Campaign.} Finally, the algorithmic numbers of
\cref{ch:eval} should be complemented by a networked, database-backed campaign (persistent storage,
wide-area latencies, and concurrent load) to characterize the costs a real service pays, which the
comparison with database-bound systems (\cref{sec:eval-compare}) suggests are dominated by persistence
at scale. Such a campaign should also include a parameter-sensitivity sweep over the epoch cadence
and the catch-up window, and the client-side energy and CPU profile on mobile hardware, the
deployment-facing metrics the in-memory benchmarks cannot speak to. Together with the warning-channel hardening noted above (rate limiting and signed
warning payloads), this
would close the remaining distance between the measured prototype and a production key-transparency
service with an in-band warning channel.
