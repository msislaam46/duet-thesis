\chapter{Background}\label{ch:background}

This chapter establishes the background needed to understand the design of DUET. It begins with
Signal's asynchronous key-distribution model and then examines key-transparency systems and
continuous-authentication research to derive the requirements carried into \cref{ch:design}.
\Cref{app:extended-background} provides further detail on protocol mechanics, usability evidence, and
the systems reviewed in this chapter.

\section{The Signal Protocol Stack}\label{sec:bg-signal}

Signal is an end-to-end encrypted messenger~\cite{signal-home,dowling2020nocompromise}. Its
protocol combines X3DH for asynchronous session establishment with the Double Ratchet for ongoing key
evolution~\cite{x3dh,doubleratchet}. The messaging server supplies the public pre-key material needed to start a
conversation while the recipient is offline~\cite[Sections~3.1--3.3]{x3dh}, so confidentiality depends on the
authenticity of keys obtained from the server.

\subsection{Key Material, Safety Numbers, and What They Cover}\label{ssec:bg-keymat}

Under the X3DH protocol used by Signal, a client publishes its long-term identity key, a signed
pre-key, and optional one-time pre-keys to the messaging server~\cite[Sections~2.4 and 3.2]{x3dh}. The Safety
Number authenticates the parties' identity keys and account identifiers through an out-of-band
comparison~\cite{safetynumbers}. A signed pre-key is authenticated indirectly by its identity-key
signature, while one-time pre-keys are unsigned. The ceremony therefore authenticates the long-term
identity binding rather than every public value used in one session. \Cref{sec:ext-signal} provides the
full key-material notation and coverage.

\subsection{The Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH) Protocol}\label{ssec:bg-x3dh}

X3DH lets Alice establish a shared secret $\sharedS$ with an offline Bob by combining identity,
ephemeral, signed-pre-key, and optional one-time-pre-key Diffie--Hellman terms~\cite[Sections~2.4 and 3.3]{x3dh}.
The resulting secret initializes the Double Ratchet~\cite{doubleratchet} and is also the input from which DUET
derives conversation labels. Alice verifies Bob's signed pre-key under the supplied identity key and
aborts if that signature fails~\cite[Section~3.3]{x3dh}.

When Bob's genuine one-time pre-key is absent or withheld, an attacker that substitutes Bob's identity
key and a signed pre-key valid under that identity can derive the resulting secret and impersonate
Bob. Replacing only the signed pre-key fails the signature check, while replacing only the one-time
pre-key normally causes the parties to derive different secrets. DUET targets the coordinated
substitution, whose full derivation and message sequence are in \cref{sec:ext-signal}.

\subsection{The Double Ratchet Protocol}\label{ssec:bg-ratchet}

The Double Ratchet evolves a shared secret established by X3DH or PQXDH into per-message keys and
provides forward secrecy~\cite{doubleratchet,pqxdh}. Once the adversary becomes passive, it can
also provide post-compromise recovery~\cite{doubleratchet}. It does not re-authenticate the
session-establishment keys, so authentication still depends on an authentic initial binding. \Cref{sec:ext-ratchet} explains the key-schedule details.

\subsection{Safety Numbers and the Verification Gap}\label{ssec:bg-gap}

To detect man-in-the-middle key substitution, Signal provides Safety Numbers for out-of-band
comparison and an optional Automatic Key Verification (AKV). AKV checks the peer's identity-key
binding against Signal's transparency service, but requires Signal to have the contact's phone
number. When AKV is unavailable, including for chats started with a username, Signal recommends
manual Safety Number comparison. Its documented workflow returns the result to the verifying
client and does not describe a way to warn the peer~\cite{signal-akv}.

Usability evidence also limits reliance on manual verification. Across unguided trials in one study,
only 14\% of pair--application trials succeeded, and only two of twelve pairs completed the ceremony
in any tested application~\cite[Section~5]{vaziripour2017}. Instructions improved
success~\cite[Section~5]{vaziripour2017}, but even instructed users took an average of 3.2 minutes to
find the ceremony and another 7.8 minutes to complete
it~\cite[p.~36]{vaziripour2017}. MoDUSA authenticates evolving protocol state but retains a
user-initiated comparison, while ACKA replaces that comparison with an assumed secure
log~\cite{dowling2021meta,dowling2023acka}. The supporting measurements are reported in
\cref{sec:ext-usability}.

These limitations lead to three requirements for DUET. It must detect coordinated substitution of
session-establishment keys, avoid reliance on a rarely completed verification ceremony, and communicate
a detected substitution to the peer instead of leaving the result local to the key owner.

\section{Secure Transparency Logs}\label{sec:bg-logs}

Transparency logs commit to a signed, append-only sequence of
states~\cite[Sections~2.1.4 and 4.10]{rfc9162}. Applied to a key directory, they let clients verify
returned bindings and monitor later states for unexpected keys or an inconsistent
history~\cite{melara2015coniks,chase2019seemless}.

\subsection{Commitments and Merkle Log Trees}\label{ssec:bg-merkle}

A Merkle root commits to every leaf under a collision-resistant hash. An inclusion proof shows that a
record belongs to the committed tree, while a consistency proof shows that a later tree preserves
every entry in an earlier tree~\cites[Section~2.1]{rfc6962}[Sections~2.1.1--2.1.4]{rfc9162}.
Both proofs are logarithmic in the relevant tree size. Inclusion authenticates one record, while
consistency establishes append-only growth. The construction, domain separation, and proof
walkthrough are explained in \cref{sec:ext-merkle}.

\subsection{Key Transparency Requirements}\label{ssec:bg-ct2kt}

Key transparency uses verifiable commitments for identity-to-key
bindings~\cite[Sections~1 and 3]{melara2015coniks} and can use a verifiable
random function (VRF) to derive privacy-preserving lookup labels that resist
offline enumeration by outsiders~\cite[Section~1]{rfc9381}. The VRF does not
hide the client's query from the operator. Moreover, concealing the directory's scale
requires additional mechanisms, such as the dummy entries used by
CONIKS~\cite[Section~2.2]{melara2015coniks}.

Verified Merkle proofs show that a directory response matches a committed state, but they do not
show whether every observer received that same state~\cite[Section~3.3]{ietf-keytrans-architecture}.
A malicious operator can present Alice and Bob with different but internally consistent histories.
Detecting this split view requires comparing log commitments observed by different parties or through
separate channels~\cites[Section~4.1.4]{melara2015coniks}[Section~10.2]{ietf-keytrans-protocol}.
Existing comparison mechanisms are discussed further in \cref{sec:ext-splitview}.

\subsection{The IETF KeyTrans Architecture}\label{ssec:bg-keytrans}

The IETF Key Transparency protocol combines a VRF-indexed prefix tree with a log tree that records
successive prefix-tree roots~\cites[Section~8]{ietf-keytrans-architecture}
[Sections~3.3--3.4 and 11.7]{ietf-keytrans-protocol}. It defines search and monitoring operations
along with third-party auditing~\cites[Sections~3.1 and 4.2]{ietf-keytrans-architecture}
[Sections~13.1--13.4 and 15.2]{ietf-keytrans-protocol}. Signal's key-transparency server uses the protocol
with changes for its application requirements~\cite{signal-kt-server}. The protocol uses the
Reasonable Monitoring Window to select regularly spaced distinguished log entries and specifies
separate contact- and owner-monitoring algorithms~\cite[Sections~6.1 and
8.2--8.3]{ietf-keytrans-protocol}.

\section{Verifiable Key Directories for Key Transparency}\label{sec:bg-vkd}

In key transparency, a verifiable key directory (VKD) provides an authenticated mapping from user
identifiers to public keys, allowing clients to verify membership and non-membership answers against
a public commitment. The authors of SEEMless formalized this abstraction through completeness,
soundness, and simulation-based privacy~\cite[Section~2]{chase2019seemless}.

\subsection{Cryptographic Building Blocks}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-blocks}

Key-transparency constructions use combinations of VRFs, Merkle directories, and zero-knowledge
sets to provide private lookup labels and verifiable directory answers. CONIKS and SEEMless combine
private labels with sparse or path-compressed Merkle
directories~\cite{melara2015coniks,chase2019seemless}. Zero-knowledge-set variants further limit
what proofs reveal beyond the queried binding. SEEMless uses an append-only ZKS (aZKS), Parakeet an
ordered ZKS (oZKS), and ELEKTRA a rotatable ZKS
(RZKS)~\cites[Sections~3--6]{chase2019seemless}[Section~III]{malvai2023parakeet}
[Section~3.1]{len2023elektra}[Section~3]{chen2022rzks}.
Merkle\textsuperscript{2}, a general transparency-log system with applications to public-key
transparency, uses chronological Merkle trees in the outer layer, with a prefix tree corresponding
to each internal node~\cite[Sections~I, IV, and~XI]{hu2021merkle2}.
\Cref{sec:ext-vkd-blocks} explains the structural differences and terminology.

\subsection{Academic and Deployed Systems}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-academic}

Academic key-transparency designs differ in monitoring, storage, privacy, and deployment. CONIKS
uses private lookup indices and client
self-monitoring~\cite[Sections~3.4 and 4.1.3]{melara2015coniks}, while SEEMless formalizes VKDs and
makes monitoring depend mainly on key
updates~\cite[Sections~1--2 and 4.2]{chase2019seemless}. Parakeet combines history compaction with a
witness quorum~\cite[Sections~IV--V]{malvai2023parakeet}, and ELEKTRA supports multi-device key
histories and privacy recovery after server
compromise~\cite[Sections~1, 2.1, and 3.1]{len2023elektra}. OPTIKS reduces the cost of monitoring an
unchanged key to a single non-membership proof~\cite[p.~4356]{optiks2024}. PRISM uses
whole-directory verification~\cite[Sections~1 and 4]{pusch2025prism}. Across the surveyed designs,
clients, auditors, or witness quorums perform verification, but none defines a conversation-specific
peer-warning channel for an individual key substitution.

A comparison with ELEKTRA and PRISM clarifies this peer-warning gap. A full account reset in
ELEKTRA invokes \texttt{AddFirstKey}, which appends another \texttt{AddFirst} statement that
contacts can detect when they query the keychain. The paper recommends that client applications
show an urgent warning for this event~\cite[Section~2.2]{len2023elektra}. PRISM frames the wider problem as reliance on external checks
such as client gossip and third-party auditing~\cite[p.~6]{pusch2025prism}. It also proposes
embedding commitments in encrypted messages as an additional gossip
safeguard~\cite[p.~5]{pusch2025prism}. A warning about one substituted key therefore remains
distinct from both mechanisms.

CONIKS is the principal baseline for this thesis because it combines private lookup indices, signed
tree roots, and client self-monitoring~\cite[Sections~3.2--3.3 and
4.1.3]{melara2015coniks}. A client monitors its own binding every epoch and compares the provider's
signed tree root with one observed by an auditor to detect
equivocation~\cite[Sections~4.1.3--4.1.4]{melara2015coniks}. CONIKS relies on auditors for this
comparison, while direct exchange between clients remains future
work~\cite[Section~4.1.4, footnote~5]{melara2015coniks}. A returning client that misses several
epochs must also perform a series of checks to confirm that its last verified inclusion proofs and
signed tree roots remain consistent with the recent proofs~\cite[pp.~7--8]{melara2015coniks}. This
missed-epoch procedure provides the baseline for DUET's windowed catch-up evaluation.

Beyond academic designs, key transparency is already used in production communication services and
explored in research and development projects. WhatsApp, Signal, Proton, Google, and Apple use
different approaches to client monitoring, external auditing, and signed-commitment
comparison~\cite{whatsapp2023whitepaper,signal-kt-server,goebel2023proton,google-kt,apple-ckv}.
Moreover, Meta's Auditable Key Directory (AKD) implementation was independently reviewed, and
retesting confirmed that all findings were fixed~\cite{nccgroup2023akd}. However, none of these
sources describes the conversation-specific peer-warning channel defined in this thesis for an
individual key substitution. Their mechanisms and limitations are examined in
\cref{sec:ext-vkd-survey} and summarized in \cref{tab:bg-compare}.

The surveyed systems also make different engineering choices. The WhatsApp whitepaper reports
publicly verifiable audit proofs and states that key-history requests were not yet enabled in its
clients~\cite[pp.~5--6 and 11]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. Signal's repository states that its
implementation uses the IETF protocol with application-specific modifications, and its verifier
requires a fixed-length prefix copath~\cite{signal-kt-server}. ProtonKT combines client
self-auditing and external auditing with Certificate
Transparency~\cite[pp.~39, 44, 47]{goebel2023proton}. The Proton thesis also describes the
investigation of KT warnings as an open user-experience
challenge~\cite[pp.~81--82]{goebel2023proton}.

\section{The ACKA (Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement) Framework}\label{sec:bg-acka}

The ACKA construction proposed by Dowling and Hale combines continuous key agreement (CKA),
Continuous Entity Authentication (CEA), and an abstract secure
log~\cite[pp.~20--21]{dowling2023acka}. At each epoch, CEA derives a public label from the
pre-update state and a fingerprint that authenticates the public update produced by the
CKA~\cite[pp.~13--14]{dowling2023acka}. The sender stores the label and fingerprint in the log,
and the receiver queries the expected label before accepting the
update~\cite[pp.~7--8]{dowling2023acka}. A substituted update either produces conflicting
fingerprints at the expected label or leaves no matching fingerprint
there~\cite[pp.~7--8]{dowling2023acka}. The required security notions are $\CEAsec$ for
CEA~\cite[p.~18]{dowling2023acka} and $\Logsec$ for the
log~\cite[pp.~10--11]{dowling2023acka}.
\Cref{fig:bg-acka} summarizes this composition.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{figures/tikz/fig-bg-acka}
\caption[ACKA composition]{ACKA composes CKA and CEA with the abstract
secure-log interface $\Log$, whose required security notion is $\Logsec$. Adapted from Dowling and
Hale~\cite{dowling2023acka}.}
\label{fig:bg-acka}
\end{figure}

ACKA targets an active channel adversary and provides continuous authentication when CEA satisfies
$\CEAsec$ and the secure log satisfies $\Logsec$~\cite[pp.~6--7]{dowling2023acka}. It begins from
a common initial state, corresponding to correctly assumed trust on first use or authenticated
initial keying material~\cite[p.~6]{dowling2023acka}. It then authenticates each subsequent CKA
update in sequence through a CEA label and fingerprint~\cite[pp.~7--8]{dowling2023acka}. The
construction presented in the paper provides one-sided detection, in which the receiver detects a
forgery~\cite[p.~21]{dowling2023acka}. Its secure-log interface supports consistency verification
between views~\cite[pp.~10--11]{dowling2023acka}. However, the paper does not define a third-party
auditor for comparing those views~\cite[p.~21]{dowling2023acka}. The formal CKA definition,
adversary model, secure-log construction, and one-sided detection result are examined further in
\cref{sec:ext-acka}.

The ACKA paper formalizes the per-epoch CEA construction and evaluates it through security proofs, but
does not report a software implementation or empirical
evaluation~\cite[Sections~4--6]{dowling2023acka}. Moreover, it leaves $\Log$ as a generic secure-log
interface~\cite[pp.~10, 21]{dowling2023acka}. The paper therefore leaves open how this interface can
be instantiated as a verifiable key directory that exposes misbehaviour by a malicious operator.

\subsection{Implications for the Design}\label{ssec:bg-implications}

The preceding comparison identifies three requirements for DUET. First, a client that detects a
substitution must be able to warn its peer. Existing systems leave detection to the key owner, an
auditor, a witness set, or a global verifier. In the cited WhatsApp configuration, a failed
automatic verification leads to manual comparison~\cite[pp.~5, 10]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}, and
Signal similarly recommends Safety Number comparison when Automatic Key Verification is
unavailable~\cite{signal-akv}. ELEKTRA recommends warning contacts after a full account
reset~\cite[Section~2.2]{len2023elektra}. DUET therefore needs a conversation-specific warning that the
peer can retrieve after reconnecting.

Second, a returning client that retains its last trusted head should verify the missed history
efficiently. CONIKS catches up by performing a series of checks after missed
epochs~\cite[pp.~7--8]{melara2015coniks}. Thus, DUET should use one consistency proof from that
trusted head to the current head to cover the complete missed window.

Third, DUET should detect split-view equivocation. A locally verified proof shows that one response
matches a committed state, but it cannot show whether another client received a different state.
Detection therefore requires comparing signed heads observed by different parties or through
separate channels. Together, the three requirements motivate the dual-index warning, windowed
consistency, and auditor mechanisms in \cref{ch:design}.

Finally, ACKA provides the framework for authenticating evolving session state. DUET should
instantiate ACKA's generic $\Log$ interface with the verifiable directory and retain its per-epoch
CEA construction for successive CKA
updates~\cite[pp.~10, 13--14, 20--21]{dowling2023acka}.
