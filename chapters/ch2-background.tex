\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:background}

In this chapter we introduce the building blocks that the remainder of the
thesis assembles and place this work in the context of existing research. We
begin with the Signal protocol stack and the precise sense in which it leaves
public keys unverified (\cref{sec:bg-signal}). We then introduce transparency
logs, the mechanism used by the key-transparency literature to make a key server
accountable (\cref{sec:bg-logs}), before examining the verifiable key directory
(VKD) abstraction and the academic and deployed systems against which this work
is compared (\cref{sec:bg-vkd}). We then turn to the Authenticated Continuous
Key Agreement (ACKA) framework, which addresses a complementary threat model
concerned with maintaining authentication guarantees throughout long-lived
asynchronous communication (\cref{sec:bg-acka}). Finally, we synthesize the
findings of the literature (\cref{sec:bg-compare}), identify the open problems
that remain for key transparency in asynchronous messaging
(\cref{sec:bg-open}), and position the contributions of this thesis against
those gaps (\cref{sec:bg-contrib}).

The two questions that motivate this chapter are simple to state but difficult
to answer in practice. First, how do Alice and Bob know that the public keys
returned by a messaging service genuinely belong to one another rather than
being substituted by a malicious or compromised server? Second, how can Alice
and Bob obtain continuous cryptographic assurance that the keys used throughout
an asynchronous conversation remain authentic, even when devices are
compromised, messages are delayed, or supporting infrastructure behaves
maliciously? The first question motivates the study of key transparency and
verifiable key directories, whereas the second is captured formally by the
Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement (ACKA) framework that underpins modern
analyses of secure messaging systems.

\section{The Signal protocol stack}\label{sec:bg-signal}

In this section we introduce the cryptographic protocol that secures the
conversations this thesis is concerned with, and we isolate the exact weakness
it leaves open.

In end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) messaging, each user holds a secret key that
never leaves their device and a corresponding public key that anyone may use to
encrypt to them or to verify their signatures. To start a conversation, Alice
must obtain Bob's public key from somewhere, and in every deployed messenger
that ``somewhere'' is a server the provider operates. The arrangement is, in
effect, a phonebook that the operator controls. Alice looks up ``Bob'' and
trusts the number written next to the name. If the operator writes its own key
next to Bob's name, it can read everything Alice sends while both parties
believe the channel is secure. The authenticity of the looked-up public key is
therefore the single assumption on which confidentiality rests, and it is the
assumption this thesis is about.

\subsection{What Signal is and why it matters}\label{ssec:bg-whatis}

Signal is an end-to-end encrypted messaging protocol designed to provide
confidentiality, authentication, forward secrecy, and post-compromise security
for asynchronous communication. Unlike traditional secure channels, where both
parties must be online simultaneously, Signal allows users to initiate
conversations while recipients are offline through the use of server-hosted
pre-keys. The protocol has become influential well beyond the Signal
application itself: variants of its design have been adopted by WhatsApp, Google
Messages, and other large-scale messaging platforms~\cite{doubleratchet}.

Signal is not a single cryptographic primitive but a protocol stack. Session
establishment is performed using the Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH) key
agreement protocol, while ongoing communication is protected using the Double
Ratchet algorithm. Together these mechanisms provide strong confidentiality
guarantees against passive adversaries and support recovery from compromise
through continuous key evolution.

The security of the entire construction, however, depends on one assumption
external to the cryptographic protocols themselves: that users receive authentic
public keys when initiating communication. Signal's server distributes the
public keys required for X3DH~\cite{signaldocs}, and although the protocol provides a manual
verification mechanism known as the Safety Number, it does not, on its own,
guarantee that the keys supplied by the server are authentic. Understanding
precisely where this assumption enters the protocol stack is therefore necessary
before examining the key transparency mechanisms introduced later in this
chapter. Signal therefore serves as both a case study and the deployment context
through which this thesis examines the broader problem of public-key
verification in secure messaging.

\subsection{Key material, Safety Numbers, and what they cover}\label{ssec:bg-keymat}

To support asynchronous communication, Signal requires users to publish several
types of public key material. A user $U$ generates a long-term identity key pair
$(\pkid{U},\skid{U})$, retains the secret key $\skid{U}$ locally, and publishes
the corresponding public identity key $\pkid{U}$. The user also publishes a
medium-term signed pre-key $\spk{U}$ together with a signature $\sig{\spk{U}}$
produced under $\skid{U}$, and a batch of single-use one-time pre-keys
$\opk{U}{i}$. These public values are uploaded to the server so that a sender
can initiate a session while the recipient is offline.

Historically, Signal's principal built-in defence against a server that
substitutes one of these keys is the Safety Number: a 60-digit fingerprint,
derived from the two parties' long-term identity keys together with their stable
account identifiers, that users are expected
to compare out of band, by scanning a QR code in person, or reading digits
aloud over a separate channel~\cite{safetynumbers}. More recently, Signal has introduced Automatic
Key Verification (AKV), a key-transparency-based mechanism discussed in
\cref{ssec:bg-gap}. Understanding the scope of the Safety Number remains
important because it illustrates the original authentication model on which much
of the literature was based.

The decisive observation, which \cref{ssec:bg-gap} develops, is one of
\emph{coverage}. The Safety Number is a function of the identity keys
$\pkid{A}$ and $\pkid{B}$ and the parties' account identifiers only, and
therefore authenticates the long-term
identities of the communicating parties and nothing else directly. The signed
pre-key is authenticated only \emph{indirectly}, by its signature
$\sig{\spk{}}$ under the identity key, so it is authentic relative to an
authentic identity key but is not itself covered by the Safety Number. The
one-time pre-keys are unsigned and are not authenticated at all. What no part of
this machinery provides is authentication of the particular \emph{session}: the
specific keys actually combined in a given handshake are never checked together.
This per-session gap, rather than a mere shortfall in the number of keys
fingerprinted, is what the remainder of the thesis addresses. \Cref{tab:bg-keymat}
summarizes the key material each party holds.

The key material described above is consumed directly by Signal's
session-establishment protocol. We therefore further examine how the X3DH
protocol combines these keys to derive the initial shared secret from which all
subsequent communication keys are generated.

\subsection{The Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH) protocol}\label{ssec:bg-x3dh}

Having introduced the public key material that Signal users publish, we can now
examine how that material is used during session establishment. The Extended
Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH) protocol allows Alice to derive a shared secret
(denoted by $\sharedS$) with Bob even when Bob is offline, by combining Alice's
keys with the identity, signed pre-key, and one-time pre-key material published
by Bob.

Following the notation used in the X3DH specification~\cite[\S2.4]{x3dh}, we
denote identity keys by $\xIK{}$, signed pre-keys by $\xSPK{}$, one-time
pre-keys by $\xOPK{}$, and ephemeral keys by $\xEK{}$. Hence we write $\xIK{U}$ for a
user's identity key, $\xEK{A}$ for Alice's ephemeral key, $\xSPK{U}$ for a
signed pre-key, and $\xOPK{U}$ for a one-time pre-key, each a public key with a
corresponding private key held by its owner, so that $\xIK{U}=\pkid{U}$,
$\xSPK{U}=\spk{U}$, and $\xOPK{U}=\opk{U}{i}$. Alice generates an ephemeral key
$\xEK{A}$ and computes four Diffie--Hellman values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bg-x3dh}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{DH1}    &= \DH{\xIK{A}}{\xSPK{B}}, \\
\mathrm{DH2}    &= \DH{\xEK{A}}{\xIK{B}}, \\
\mathrm{DH3}    &= \DH{\xEK{A}}{\xSPK{B}}, \\
\mathrm{DH4}^{*} &= \DH{\xEK{A}}{\xOPK{B}}
   \qquad\text{(if the bundle contains a one-time pre-key)}, \\
\sharedS &= \KDF\!\left(\mathrm{DH1}\concat\mathrm{DH2}\concat\mathrm{DH3}
   \concat\mathrm{DH4}^{*}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\DH{X}{Y}$ denotes the Diffie--Hellman shared secret derived from the key
pairs associated with $X$ and $Y$, following the notation of the X3DH
specification. The notation suppresses whether the public or private component
of each key pair is used by a given party during the computation. $\KDF$ is a
key-derivation function, $\concat$ is byte-string concatenation, and $\sharedS$
is the resulting shared secret, which will later serve as an input to the
constructions developed in \cref{ch:design}. The derivation of \cref{eq:bg-x3dh}
is the point at which key authenticity must hold: if an adversary substitutes
$\xIK{B}$, every downstream secret is computed against the wrong party.

The identity keys enter the shared secret through $\mathrm{DH1}$ and
$\mathrm{DH2}$: $\mathrm{DH1}$ combines Alice's identity key with Bob's signed
pre-key, and $\mathrm{DH2}$ combines Bob's identity key with Alice's ephemeral
key, so that both parties' long-term identities are bound into session
establishment. $\mathrm{DH3}$ and $\mathrm{DH4}^{*}$ incorporate Bob's signed
pre-key and one-time pre-key material, contributing forward secrecy, and the
fourth term is omitted when no one-time pre-key is
available~\cite[\S3.3]{x3dh}.

The property that matters for this thesis is that $\sharedS$ depends on all of
the public key material used during session establishment, not only the identity
keys: three distinct classes of key material (identity keys, signed pre-keys,
and one-time pre-keys) contribute directly to its derivation. Consequently, the
authenticity of every key in Bob's published bundle affects the resulting
session, and if an attacker can substitute any of these values during bundle
distribution, the security guarantees of the resulting session may be
compromised. This is precisely the gap that \cref{ssec:bg-gap} develops when it
evaluates the scope of Signal's authentication mechanisms. \Cref{fig:bg-x3dh}
shows the full message sequence, from Bob publishing his bundle to Alice's initial
message.

Signal's post-quantum variant, PQXDH, additionally mixes in a key-encapsulation
secret so that traffic recorded today cannot be decrypted by a future quantum
adversary (a ``harvest-now, decrypt-later'' attacker)~\cite{pqxdh}.

In Signal, the shared secret $\sharedS$ is not used directly to encrypt
application data. Instead, it serves as the starting point for the Double
Ratchet protocol, which continuously evolves cryptographic state throughout the
lifetime of the conversation. We next examine how Signal transforms this initial
shared secret into per-message encryption keys.

\subsection{The Double Ratchet protocol}\label{ssec:bg-ratchet}

Once the shared secret $\sharedS$ has been established by X3DH or PQXDH, Signal
hands control to the Double Ratchet protocol. Whereas X3DH is responsible for
bootstrapping trust and deriving an initial secret, the Double Ratchet protocol
is responsible for maintaining confidentiality throughout the remainder of the
conversation. It combines a Diffie--Hellman ``ratchet'', in which the parties
periodically exchange new ephemeral public keys, with symmetric key-derivation
chains that advance a root key into per-message keys~\cite{doubleratchet}. This
gives two properties: forward secrecy, meaning that compromise of a current key
does not expose past messages, because the chain only moves forward, and
post-compromise security, meaning that the session can heal after a compromise
once the attacker stops interfering, because a fresh Diffie--Hellman ratchet
step re-randomizes the state. Post-compromise security, however, is guaranteed
only against an adversary who becomes passive after the compromise. It offers no
guarantee against one who remains active. We return to this active-adversary
setting in \cref{sec:bg-acka}. \Cref{fig:bg-ratchet} shows how the Diffie--Hellman
ratchet and the symmetric-key chains interact.

root key, which seeds a symmetric chain producing one message key $mk_i$ per

Although the Double Ratchet algorithm continuously refreshes encryption keys, it
does not continuously re-authenticate the public keys from which the session
originated. The security guarantees it provides therefore assume that the key
material used during session establishment was authentic in the first place.
This returns us to the question left open in \cref{ssec:bg-keymat}: how do users
know that the public keys supplied by the server were genuine? Signal's answer
is the Safety Number mechanism, whose limitations motivate the remainder of this
chapter.

\subsection{Safety Numbers and the verification gap}\label{ssec:bg-gap}

The mechanisms described in the preceding subsections establish confidential
communication and continuously refresh encryption keys, but they do not
themselves establish that the public keys used during session initiation were
authentic. Signal therefore provides two verification mechanisms. The first is
the traditional Safety Number ceremony introduced in \cref{ssec:bg-keymat}, in
which users compare a fingerprint derived from their long-term identity keys
over an independent channel. The second is Automatic Key Verification (AKV), a
more recent, optional feature built on key transparency: when the user selects
``Verify automatically'' from the safety-number screen, the client checks the
public keys it holds for a contact against Signal's transparency log and
displays an ``Encryption verified'' indicator when that check
succeeds~\cite{signal-akv}.

The introduction of AKV is significant because it demonstrates that key
transparency has moved beyond research prototypes and into large-scale
deployment. Signal's own documentation, however, highlights limitations that are
directly relevant to this thesis. AKV is unavailable in several situations,
including chats initiated through usernames, contacts that are not discoverable
through phone numbers, and cases where a device stores outdated contact
information~\cite{signal-akv}. More fundamentally, AKV does not verify real-world
identity and cannot prevent impersonation or account compromise~\cite{signal-akv}.
Like other key-transparency deployments discussed later in this chapter, it
verifies a user's own view of a binding rather than providing a mechanism
through which one user can actively warn another that a key substitution has been
detected.

Despite these mechanisms, the authentication problem remains incomplete. The
remaining gap has two dimensions: a structural gap arising from the protocol's
authentication model and a human gap arising from the usability of verification
ceremonies.

On the structural side, Dowling and Hale observe that authentication based on
long-term identity keys authenticates only the state of the protocol at session
initiation and not its subsequent evolution~\cite[p.~4]{dowling2023acka}. The
same limitation applies to trust-on-first-use systems, where authenticity is
established once and then assumed thereafter~\cite[p.~4]{dowling2023acka}. Their
earlier MoDUSA construction
addresses this by deriving authentication values throughout the lifetime of the
conversation, allowing participants to detect state divergence caused by an
active man-in-the-middle attacker~\cite{dowling2021meta}. However, MoDUSA still
depends on user-initiated verification. Authentication information exists, but
users must actively compare it. As a result, a continuous key agreement provides
no automatic re-authentication during long-lived conversations. Dowling and Hale
argue that this can leave an active adversary undetected for years: in a stylized
three-year scenario in which the compromise occurs after the first year, this
exposes up to two years of communication, against the single (assumed one-hour)
session that a TLS deployment would expose~\cite[p.~3]{dowling2023acka}.

The human dimension of the problem is equally important because, in the many
chats where AKV is unavailable, verification still falls back to a manual user
ceremony. In practice, users rarely
perform it correctly. In a paired-participant usability study of the
authentication ceremony across WhatsApp, Viber and Facebook Messenger, only two
of twelve pairs located and completed the ceremony when simply
asked to make sure no one else could read their messages, an overall success
rate of 14\% across all pairs and applications~\cite[p.~30]{vaziripour2017}.
Brief, app-generic instructions about the need to compare keys raised the
overall success rate to 79\%, as high as 96\% for Viber~\cite[p.~29]{vaziripour2017},
but even when prompted the ceremony was slow: 3.2 minutes to find and a further
7.8 minutes to complete, on average~\cite[p.~36]{vaziripour2017}, which the
authors call unsatisfactory. Users also lacked the threat model the ceremony
defends: of the participants who did not mention key verification, 88\% relied
on recognizing features of their partner such as voice or
appearance~\cite[p.~39]{vaziripour2017}, and across the attacker types they
named, the man-in-the-middle ``was not explicitly mentioned as a possible attack
vector''~\cite[p.~40]{vaziripour2017}. The study's own authors draw the
conclusion this thesis starts from, that a system like CONIKS ``may help to
automate the process of discovering another person's key without relying on a
single trusted party, while also providing non-equivocation so that key servers
cannot deceive users''~\cite[p.~40]{vaziripour2017}.

In conclusion, these findings reveal a persistent authentication gap. The public
keys used during session establishment are distributed by a party that could
substitute them; verification mechanisms remain dependent on assumptions about
the consistency of directory views and the availability of auditing
infrastructure; authentication is tied primarily to key bindings rather than the
evolving protocol state, and manual user verification remains uncommon in
practice. The remainder of this chapter examines the principal approaches
proposed to address these limitations, beginning with key transparency and
verifiable key directories before turning to the complementary perspective
offered by the Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement (ACKA) framework, and
finally to the contributions of this thesis, which propose a unified system
bridging the KT and ACKA frameworks.

\section{Secure Transparency Logs}\label{sec:bg-logs}

Since the keys for session establishment are distributed by a server that could
substitute them, the server must be made accountable for what it hands out. Key
transparency meets this demand with one organizing idea, that the server should
be forced to publish an irrevocable, public record of the keys it serves, so
that handing Alice a forged key for Bob becomes an act the server can be caught
having committed in writing. This section develops the data structures that make
such a record possible, in the order a reader needs them: the cryptographic
notion of a commitment and the Merkle tree that realizes it
(\cref{ssec:bg-merkle}); the origin of these structures in Certificate
Transparency, and the two changes required to carry them into messaging
(\cref{ssec:bg-ct2kt}), and the architecture the IETF is now standardizing
around them (\cref{ssec:bg-keytrans}).

\subsection{Commitments and Merkle log trees}\label{ssec:bg-merkle}

The transparency systems examined throughout this chapter are built from a small
hierarchy of cryptographic abstractions. At the foundation is a
collision-resistant hash function. Hash functions enable commitments: compact
values that bind a publisher to a larger body of data. Merkle trees then
organize many such commitments into a structure that supports efficient
verification. Transparency logs, in turn, use Merkle trees to make server
behaviour publicly auditable. Because each layer depends on the one beneath it,
we begin with the notion of a commitment.

A commitment is a short fingerprint of a body of data: small enough to publish
or repeat widely, yet so tightly bound to the data that altering any part of it
(adding, deleting, or modifying even a single entry) changes the
fingerprint. A collision-resistant hash function $\Hash$ makes this precise.
$\Hash$ maps an input of arbitrary length to a fixed-length output, and
collision resistance means that no efficient adversary can find two distinct
inputs that produce the same output. Publishing $\Hash(D)$ therefore commits the
publisher to $D$. To later substitute a different $D'\neq D$ while retaining the
same commitment would require producing a hash collision, which collision
resistance is assumed to prevent.

This binding property is the source of accountability in transparency systems.
Once a server publishes a commitment to a directory state, any later attempt to
rewrite that state requires either producing a hash collision or presenting a
different commitment, both of which are detectable.

A single hash binds an entire directory state, but it does not support efficient
verification of individual entries nor efficient evolution over time. Merkle
trees address both problems simultaneously. A Merkle tree is a binary tree in
which each leaf contains the hash of one record and each internal node contains
the hash of its two children, $\Hash(\text{left}\concat\text{right})$, where
$\concat$ denotes concatenation. The value at the root, commonly called the tree
head or root hash, is therefore a commitment to the entire
collection~\cite{rfc6962,rfc9162}. Because every internal node depends
recursively on the values beneath it, the root depends on every record in the
tree while remaining only a single hash long.

The specifications used by transparency systems deliberately distinguish leaves
from internal nodes by prepending different one-byte prefixes before hashing.
This form of domain separation prevents a leaf from ever being interpreted as an
internal node and vice versa, eliminating an otherwise subtle class of
structural ambiguities. This structure, due originally to Merkle, is the backbone
of Certificate Transparency, discussed in the following section, and of the RFCs
that specify it, RFC~6962 and its revision RFC~9162.

Merkle trees become useful because they support two verification operations that
recur throughout the remainder of this chapter: proving that an entry exists and
proving that history has not been rewritten. An inclusion proof answers the
question ``is this record in the log, at this position?'' without shipping the
whole log: it consists of the sibling hashes along the path from the record's
leaf up to the root, and the verifier recomputes the root from the record and
those siblings and compares it against the published tree head. For a tree of
$N$ leaves that path has length $\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$, so the proof is
logarithmic in the size of the directory rather than linear~\cite[\S2.1.3]{rfc9162}.
A consistency proof answers a different question (``is the log I see now an
append-only extension of the one I saw before?'') by revealing the few
internal nodes that let a verifier confirm that an earlier tree head is
contained in a later one, with nothing previously committed modified, deleted,
or reordered. A consistency proof is also logarithmic in the size of the
log~\cite[\S2.1.4]{rfc9162}. The two proofs serve the client in equally
important ways: an inclusion proof convinces a client that its own key is
recorded correctly now, while a consistency proof convinces it that the server
has not quietly rewritten the past between visits. \Cref{fig:bg-merkle}
illustrates an inclusion proof and the copath a verifier recomputes from.

The logarithmic size of inclusion and consistency proofs is one of the principal
reasons Merkle trees are practical for scalability across the Internet. However,
these costs are measured per proof. A client that has been offline for an
extended period may need to validate a sequence of updates accumulated during
its absence, causing the total verification cost to grow with the number of
missed epochs. This distinction between per-proof efficiency and long-term
monitoring cost becomes important later, when discussing mobile clients and the
monitoring mechanisms used by key-transparency systems.

\subsection{From Certificate Transparency to Key Transparency}\label{ssec:bg-ct2kt}

Data structures such as Merkle trees were not invented for messaging. They were
first deployed at scale by Certificate Transparency (CT), the system that holds
the web's certificate authorities accountable. CT requires that every
certificate a CA issues be appended to a public, append-only Merkle log, so that
independent monitors and auditors can watch the logs and cross-check their tree
heads; a CA that issues a fraudulent certificate can no longer hide it, because
browsers that enforce a CT policy reject certificates lacking proof of logging,
and a logged certificate is visible to the domain it falsely
names~\cite{rfc6962,rfc9162}. RFC~6962 specified the original design and
RFC~9162 its current revision, and both are built from exactly the Merkle log
tree, inclusion proof, and consistency proof of the previous subsection.

The success of Certificate Transparency naturally raises the question of whether
the same accountability mechanism can be applied to public-key distribution in
messaging systems. The general framework of transparency overlays formalizes this
reuse across applications~\cite{chase2016overlays}. Key Transparency (KT) answers this question by adapting
transparency logs from certificates to user identity bindings.

The core idea remains unchanged. A server should be unable to present different
keys to different users without leaving cryptographic evidence of that
behaviour. However, moving from certificates to messaging introduces
requirements that Certificate Transparency was never designed to satisfy. In
particular, messaging systems must protect the privacy of their users and
prevent adversaries from enumerating the contents of the directory.

Two challenges set the messaging setting apart and shape the systems discussed
in this chapter. The first challenge is privacy. Certificate Transparency logs
are intentionally public because certificates are public objects. A messaging
directory, by contrast, contains user identities and associated public keys. A
transparency mechanism that exposed the entire directory would reveal
information about who uses the service and potentially who communicates with
whom. KT systems therefore require proofs that reveal information only about the
queried entry rather than the directory as a whole~\cite[p.~2]{melara2015coniks}.

The second challenge follows directly from the first. If entries were stored
under predictable labels such as usernames or simple hashes of usernames, an
adversary could enumerate the directory by generating candidate names and
checking whether they exist. KT systems therefore require labels that are
deterministic yet unpredictable. This is achieved via verifiable random
functions (VRF), standardized in RFC~9381. A VRF is a keyed function whose
outputs appear pseudorandom to anyone without the secret key while
simultaneously producing publicly verifiable proofs of correct evaluation. The
directory operator, holding the VRF secret key, maps a username to a
pseudorandom label and produces a proof that the label was computed correctly.
Clients can verify that proof against the operator's public key without being
able to compute or predict any other label~\cite{rfc9381}. The mapping must be
deterministic, ``or else the identity provider could insert multiple bindings
for a user at different locations''~\cite[p.~5]{melara2015coniks}.

It is easy to overstate what the VRF buys, and precision here matters for the
threat model that follows. The VRF hides the set of labels from outsiders, which
gives directory privacy, and binds each label immutably to its identity, which
gives label integrity, but it does not conceal a queried name from the operator,
who computes the label and so necessarily learns who is being looked up. No
deployed system surveyed in this chapter hides the queried identity from the
server, and query privacy against the operator is a separate problem, beyond the
scope of this work.

One inheritance from CT is less welcome, and it constrains every design that
follows: the split-view, or fork, attack. A consistency proof certifies only
that a log grew in an append-only way. It says nothing about whether everyone is
shown the same log. A malicious server can therefore keep two internally
consistent logs at once, presenting tree head $\logroot{A}$ to Alice and a
different $\logroot{B}$ to Bob and serving each a different key for the other,
with every local check on both sides passing. Exposing the fork requires
comparing tree heads observed from different vantage points, whether through
client gossip, anchoring tree heads in an independent transparency log, or
cross-checking by external auditors.

The split-view attack exposes a fundamental limitation of local verification. A
client can verify that a particular directory view is internally consistent, yet
still be unable to determine whether other clients are seeing the same view.
Resolving this tension between local verifiability and global consistency
becomes a central design challenge for every verifiable key directory examined
in the next section, and motivates several of the open problems discussed later
in this chapter. \Cref{fig:bg-splitview} depicts the two divergent views a
malicious server maintains.

\subsection{The IETF KeyTrans architecture}\label{ssec:bg-keytrans}

By the early 2020s, numerous academic and industrial key-transparency systems
had emerged. Although these systems differed in implementation details, most
relied on the same underlying ingredients: append-only logs, authenticated
directory structures, verifiable random functions, and independent auditing.

Following a Birds-of-a-Feather session at IETF~116 in March 2023, the IETF
chartered the Key Transparency (KEYTRANS) working group later in 2023 to
standardize a common architecture for key-transparency systems. The resulting
architecture provides the reference model against which many modern deployments
are now built and evaluated.

It is assembled from three pieces already introduced. An append-only Merkle log
tree records the directory's history over time. A per-epoch prefix tree, a
sparse Merkle tree whose leaves are the VRF-derived labels, represents the
directory's state at a single moment, an epoch being one published snapshot of
the directory. And the VRF maps identities to the labels at which they sit in
that prefix tree. Around these structures the draft defines a small interface
(update, search, monitor, and audit) and a role for third-party auditors
who cross-check tree heads to catch exactly the forks of the previous
subsection~\cite{ietf-keytrans-protocol}. Signal's production key-transparency
server implements this draft, ``with some modifications''~\cite{signal-kt-server},
using an elliptic-curve VRF from the family the standard recommends, an
independent endorsement of these design choices.

Since the architecture is assembled from standardized primitives, it is worth
recording precisely which standards the later chapters build on. The Merkle log
tree and its inclusion and consistency proofs are those of RFC~6962 and
RFC~9162~\cite{rfc6962,rfc9162}; the verifiable random function is that of
RFC~9381~\cite{rfc9381}, and the key-derivation step used later, when a label is
derived from a session secret, is HKDF, the HMAC-based extract-and-expand
key-derivation function of RFC~5869~\cite{rfc5869}. Grounding each construction
in its specification fixes the exact algorithm rather than an informal sketch of
it. The draft is also candid about one question it leaves open: it calls for
``reasonable monitoring windows'' so that intermittently-connected mobile
clients need not replay every missed update, but leaves the mechanism for
achieving them unspecified~\cite{ietf-keytrans-protocol}. This open question is
the efficiency problem we return to in later chapters.

The signed tree head deserves one further note, because it is where this work
hardens the standard design. The server signs each head with Ed25519 over the
epoch number, the tree size, the log root and a timestamp. Earlier systems such
as CONIKS instead published a \emph{linear hash chain} of signed roots, each
committing to the previous one by including the hash of its predecessor. That
chain is their mechanism for non-equivocation. The Merkle-log design used here
obtains the same property differently---the log root already commits to every
prior epoch's root, and a single consistency proof certifies append-only growth
in $O(\log n)$, whereas walking the hash chain costs one step per missed epoch.
This work keeps the Merkle-log history but borrows one element of CONIKS's signed
root that a bare tree head omits: a commitment to the provider's \emph{policy}.
The signed message additionally binds a hash of the configuration (the VRF
public key, the signing key, the ciphersuite, the protocol version and the epoch
cadence) so that a malicious server cannot silently substitute any of these
parameters between epochs, or serve different ones to different clients, without
breaking the signature for a client that recomputes the commitment from the
parameters it expects. Parameter substitution thereby becomes a signed,
monitorable, equivocation-detectable act, exactly like a forged root.

The KeyTrans architecture therefore provides a useful bridge between the
Signal-specific discussion of \cref{sec:bg-signal} and the more general
verifiable key directory constructions surveyed in \cref{sec:bg-vkd}.

\section{Verifiable Key Directories for Key Transparency}\label{sec:bg-vkd}

In this section we turn from transparency logs themselves to the systems built
upon them. Specifically, we examine the literature that uses the
transparency-log machinery of \cref{sec:bg-logs} to construct verifiable key
directories for key transparency. This literature is both the context in which
the present thesis is situated and the benchmark against which its contributions
are assessed. The limitations that repeatedly appear across these systems, and
which we survey critically throughout this section, directly motivate the
designs introduced in \cref{sec:bg-contrib}. We begin by defining the verifiable
key directory abstraction (\cref{ssec:bg-vkd-abstraction}), then review its
cryptographic foundations (\cref{ssec:bg-vkd-blocks}), the principal academic
constructions (\cref{ssec:bg-vkd-academic}), and finally the systems that have
been deployed in practice (\cref{ssec:bg-vkd-deployed}).

\subsection{The Verifiable Key Directory (VKD) abstraction}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-abstraction}

The systems surveyed in this section differ substantially in their data
structures, their privacy guarantees, and their performance, yet they all
realize the same underlying abstraction: a \emph{verifiable key directory}
(VKD). Informally, a VKD is an authenticated key--value directory that lets
clients verify the correctness of directory responses without trusting the
directory operator. Rather than returning a bare value, the directory returns a
value together with a cryptographic proof that the value is consistent with a
publicly committed directory state, and by publishing commitments to successive
states, it additionally lets clients and auditors check that the directory
evolves according to its prescribed update rules.

The abstraction was formalized most explicitly by SEEMless, which models a VKD
through a small collection of operations: \texttt{Publish}, which commits a new
directory epoch; \texttt{Query} and \texttt{QueryVer}, which retrieve and verify
a binding; \texttt{KeyHistory} and \texttt{HistoryVer}, which retrieve and verify
the history of updates associated with a label, and \texttt{Audit}, which checks
consistency between published directory states~\cite[p.~4]{chase2019seemless}.
SEEMless attaches three security goals to this interface: \emph{completeness},
requiring honest proofs to verify; \emph{soundness}, preventing a malicious
directory from producing two contradictory yet valid answers for one label; and
\emph{privacy}, formalized through simulation relative to an explicit leakage
function~\cite[p.~4]{chase2019seemless}. ELEKTRA later generalized the
abstraction to the multi-device setting as a \emph{multi-device verifiable key
directory} (MVKD), extending the model to device-key histories while retaining
analogous notions of completeness, soundness, and privacy, and formalizing for
the first time the multi-device key-chains Keybase had run in production since
2014~\cite[p.~1, p.~4]{len2023elektra}.

The abstraction intentionally leaves the implementation unspecified: different
systems realize the VKD interface with different authenticated data structures
and privacy mechanisms. The cryptographic primitives that recur throughout these
constructions are introduced next.

\subsection{Cryptographic building blocks (ZKS, sparse Merkle trees, and VRFs)}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-blocks}

Although VKD constructions differ in their design details, most are assembled
from the same small collection of cryptographic building blocks. Three
components recur throughout the literature: verifiable random functions (VRFs),
authenticated Merkle-tree directories, and zero-knowledge sets (ZKS).

\paragraph{Verifiable random functions.} The VRF was already introduced in
\cref{ssec:bg-keytrans}. Here it is enough to recall its role. The operator maps
each identity to a pseudorandom but publicly verifiable \emph{label}, so that the
set of occupied labels reveals nothing about which users are registered while
every label remains provably well-formed. In a VKD the VRF is what keys the
directory: bindings are stored not under usernames but under their VRF-derived
labels.

\paragraph{Merkle prefix trees.} Most VKDs store bindings in a sparse
authenticated dictionary indexed by these labels. Conceptually the structure is
a sparse binary Merkle tree whose leaves correspond to the whole space of
possible labels; because only a tiny fraction of labels are occupied,
implementations represent it as a \emph{Merkle prefix tree}, materializing only
the nodes on paths to existing entries. A label's bits give the path from the
root to its leaf, so a membership proof is the authentication path to that leaf
and a non-membership proof is the path to an empty (default) position. CONIKS
introduced this representation for key transparency~\cite[\S3.1]{melara2015coniks}.
Later systems commonly add \emph{Patricia-style path compression}, collapsing
chains of single-child internal nodes into a single edge. This preserves the
tree's authentication properties while reducing storage. The terms \emph{Merkle
prefix tree}, \emph{Patricia trie}, \emph{Merkle Patricia trie}, and \emph{sparse
Merkle tree} thus name closely related authenticated dictionaries that differ
chiefly in whether, and how, the tree is path-compressed.

\paragraph{Zero-knowledge sets.} Merkle trees provide authenticated membership
and non-membership proofs, but they do not by themselves provide directory
privacy. That role is played by the \emph{zero-knowledge set} (ZKS): a committed
key--value map whose membership and non-membership queries can be answered
without revealing anything further about the directory's contents. Several VKD
systems refine this primitive (SEEMless introduces an \emph{append-only} ZKS
(aZKS), Parakeet an \emph{ordered} ZKS (oZKS), and ELEKTRA a \emph{rotatable} ZKS
(RZKS)~\cite{chen2022rzks}), differing mainly in how they encode update history
and how they preserve privacy under server compromise. How each is used within a
complete system is taken up in the next subsection.

\subsection{Academic VKD constructions for KT}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-academic}

The academic VKD literature did not arrive as a set of independent proposals but
as a sequence of refinements to a shared design space, each construction
addressing a limitation of its predecessors. CONIKS introduced the basic
architecture of a privacy-preserving, auditable key directory; SEEMless
formalized the VKD abstraction and strengthened both privacy and history
verification; Parakeet improved storage efficiency and deployability, a design line
since deployed at scale by WhatsApp and independently reviewed~\cite{nccgroup2023akd}; ELEKTRA
extended the model to multi-device accounts and added privacy recovery after
server compromise, and OPTIKS, PRISM, and Merkle\textsuperscript{2} explored
alternative trade-offs between monitoring cost, auditing, and scalability. We
review the systems in roughly the order they appeared, in each case identifying
the problem the system set out to solve, its main construction, its security
contribution, and the limitations that remained---a reading that makes the
residual, common gap visible.

\subsubsection{CONIKS}
CONIKS is widely regarded as the foundational transparency-based key-verification
system for end users, and it established design principles that later VKDs
retained~\cite{melara2015coniks}. Although CONIKS predates the formal VKD
abstraction that SEEMless would later introduce, the VKD constructions that
follow inherit its core architecture of VRF-derived labels, authenticated
directory commitments, and auditable epoch-by-epoch evolution; it is best read as
the direct precursor of the VKD line rather than as a VKD itself. Its motivation
was to automate the key verification that the manual fingerprint ceremonies of
\cref{sec:bg-signal} had failed to deliver, without reintroducing a single
trusted party. Its construction stores identity bindings in a Merkle prefix tree
whose leaves sit at VRF-derived labels, and each epoch the provider publishes a
\emph{Signed Tree Root} (STR): a signed snapshot of the tree root that
hash-chains to its predecessor~\cite[\S3.3]{melara2015coniks}. A client then
audits only its own entry, downloading a logarithmic-size proof each
epoch---under 20\,kB per day even for a provider with billions of
users~\cite[p.~1]{melara2015coniks}---and checking that the key bound to its name
is the one it expects. Its security contribution is twofold. Lookups are
privacy-preserving, revealing nothing about which other users exist, and the
hash-chained STRs provide \emph{non-equivocation}, since to show two clients
different directories a provider would have to maintain two divergent STR chains
indefinitely, and any auditor comparing STRs would then obtain non-repudiable
evidence of the fork~\cite[p.~3]{melara2015coniks}. The directory records
identity-key bindings and their evolution across epochs, and nothing more. Three
limitations are significant. First, monitoring is linear in the offline period: a
client absent for $\ell$ epochs must perform $\Theta(\ell)$ work, because a
provider could insert a malicious binding and later revert it, so inspecting only
the latest STR is unsound. The proposed remedy, a per-leaf version history, would
require an auditor to perform $\Theta(n)$ work each epoch, where $n$ is the
number of changes in that epoch; the authors themselves doubt that a party
willing to perform this auditing for free will exist in every
deployment~\cite[p.~12]{melara2015coniks}. Second,
the client-to-client gossip that would let peers cross-check STRs is left as
future work~\cite[p.~7]{melara2015coniks}. Third, the guarantee is detective
rather than preventive: CONIKS exposes an attack but cannot deter a provider
willing to attack its users openly~\cite[p.~2]{melara2015coniks}.

\subsubsection{SEEMless}
SEEMless represents the transition from a deployed design pattern to a formally
defined cryptographic primitive, and that formalization is its headline
contribution: it models the \emph{verifiable key directory} as a cryptographic
primitive, with precise completeness, soundness, and simulation-based privacy
definitions that CONIKS had only described informally~\cite[p.~2]{chase2019seemless}.
The work was motivated by two weaknesses of CONIKS---the absence of such an
abstraction, and a monitoring cost that grows with the number of epochs rather
than with a user's own activity, since a CONIKS client must re-check every epoch.
Its construction is the \emph{append-only zero-knowledge set} (aZKS), built
modularly from a strong cryptographic accumulator and a VRF, and exposing commit,
query, verify, and verifiable-update operations~\cite[\S\S3, 5]{chase2019seemless}.
Keys are stored under versioned labels across two aZKS instances, an ``all'' set
and an ``old'' set, and a marker node is inserted on every $2^i$-th update, so
that proving a key is the current version costs only logarithmically in the
number of epochs~\cite[\S4.2]{chase2019seemless}; the history itself is held in a
persistent Patricia trie, so storage scales with the number of updates rather
than the number of epochs~\cite[\S6]{chase2019seemless}. SEEMless also makes
auditing a public, privacy-free role for which a single honest auditor per epoch
interval suffices, and it exhibits a concrete tracing attack by which CONIKS's
proofs leak a user's update pattern~\cite[pp.~3, 18]{chase2019seemless}. On
performance, its evaluation is entirely in-memory, committing an epoch of 2{,}000 changes
(1{,}000 new registrations and 1{,}000 key updates) in a ten-million-user
directory in 0.28\,s, with VRF evaluation excluded~\cite[pp.~11--12]{chase2019seemless}; a lookup proof,
comprising the VRF proof and the Patricia-trie authentication path, averages
about 8{,}600\,B at ten million users~\cite[p.~12]{chase2019seemless}. The
principal limitation is that detection remains contingent on user participation:
through \texttt{KeyHistory} the client retrieves the verified sequence of every
key version on its own label, and the effectiveness of detection then depends on
the user reconciling that sequence against the updates she actually
made, recognizing, for example, a new-phone or app-reinstall event as legitimate
while flagging an entry she cannot account for~\cite[p.~5]{chase2019seemless}.
Consistency of the published commitment is likewise assumed rather than solved,
deferred to an external mechanism such as a blockchain or a gossip
protocol~\cite[p.~5]{chase2019seemless}.

\subsubsection{Parakeet}
Parakeet was motivated by the storage overhead of SEEMless, whose in-memory,
append-only-forever design would require roughly 27\,TB of storage in its first
year at WhatsApp scale~\cite[p.~1]{malvai2023parakeet}. Its central construction
is the \emph{ordered zero-knowledge set} (oZKS), which collapses SEEMless's two
trees into one while still proving which version of a key is current. Realized,
like the aZKS, as a Merkle Patricia trie, the oZKS orders a key's versions within
a single tree: when Alice's $i$-th key is superseded, the server inserts an empty
leaf \texttt{Alice|i|stale} and adds the new version under \texttt{Alice|(i+1)},
pairing each leaf's value with its insertion epoch~\cite[\S III]{malvai2023parakeet}.
Because every version and its epoch live in one structure, SEEMless's separate
``old'' tree is unnecessary, and (together with a move from RAM onto a pluggable
persistent-storage backend) this yields up to an order-of-magnitude storage
reduction~\cite[\S III]{malvai2023parakeet}. To bound the unbounded growth that
append-only storage implies, Parakeet adds \emph{compaction}: a two-phase
operation that first tombstones an obsolete entry (\texttt{TombstoneElts}) and
only later deletes tombstoned entries (\texttt{DeleteElts}), under the minimal
added assumption that only entries older than a fixed parameter may be
removed---which in exchange bounds how long a misbehaving server could tamper with
a record before an auditor noticed~\cite[\S IV]{malvai2023parakeet}. For
consistency, Parakeet avoids both blockchains and gossip with a
\emph{consensusless} protocol: each epoch the provider broadcasts its new
commitment with a proof that it extends the previous one append-only, and a set
of $3f+1$ \emph{witnesses} verify and counter-sign, the provider gathering a
$2f+1$ quorum of signatures into a certificate that, by the usual
quorum-intersection argument, proves it could not have
equivocated~\cite[\S V]{malvai2023parakeet}. The witnesses also timestamp their
signatures, giving clients freshness, and withhold them while a user's update is
suppressed, bounding censorship. On performance, Parakeet creates the consistency-protocol update
notification for a 1{,}024-update batch in 47.12\,ms and a witness verifies one
in 68.70\,ms. It
sustains around 800 updates per second below 3.5\,s latency, and uses about
4.5\,GB at five million users, projected to roughly 850\,GB at one
billion~\cite[pp.~12--13]{malvai2023parakeet}. Its trade-offs are explicit: the
witness set is a new, professionally-operated trust
assumption~\cite[p.~14]{malvai2023parakeet}, its storage cost is effectively
doubled by design to support concurrent reads and
writes~\cite[p.~14]{malvai2023parakeet}, and detection remains
self-monitoring, indeed the paper rejects anonymous lookups as contrary to its
privacy goal~\cite[p.~14]{malvai2023parakeet}.

\subsubsection{ELEKTRA}
ELEKTRA extended key transparency to the multi-device accounts that real
messengers actually have, and its genuinely novel contribution is
\emph{cryptographically authenticated device addition and removal}. Rather than
treating a user as a single key, it represents each account as a
\emph{keychain}, an abstraction of Keybase's sigchains, in which every key
update, save the very first key and a full account reset, is cross-signed by an
existing device key~\cite[\S3]{len2023elektra}.
Because additions and removals are chained under the user's own device keys, a
malicious provider cannot insert a fabricated key without either forging a device
signature or compromising one of the user's devices~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra}.
This is a strictly stronger guarantee than the detect-after-the-fact model of the
prior line. The keychain is layered onto SEEMless-style versioned storage, each
update placed at a fresh VRF-derived location~\cite[p.~3]{len2023elektra}, and the
whole is built over a \emph{rotatable zero-knowledge set} (RZKS), whose
\texttt{PCSUpdate} operation periodically rotates the server's VRF key and
rebuilds the tree, restoring directory privacy after a server-state
compromise, the directory analogue of post-compromise
security~\cite[pp.~2--3]{len2023elektra}. ELEKTRA formalizes this as a new
\emph{multi-device VKD} (MVKD) primitive with completeness, extraction-based
soundness, and simulation privacy~\cite[\S2]{len2023elektra}, and makes account
resets unsuppressible, forcing a user's conversation partners to acknowledge an
explicit warning~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra}. On performance, a realistic
join-group query over 100 keychain links verifies in under a second even in a
directory of 64M links~\cite[p.~12]{len2023elektra}, though privacy rotation is
expensive: a \texttt{PCSUpdate} takes roughly 37 minutes at four million links,
dominated by tree rebuilding~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra}. Its limitations
are that the implementation is an unoptimized academic
prototype~\cite[p.~12]{len2023elektra}, the keychain guarantee relies on partners
remembering one another's prior key sets, and it remains a research prototype
rather than a deployment. ELEKTRA's reset warning is nonetheless the closest the
academic line comes to an in-band peer alarm, but it fires only on a full
account reset, not on the substitution of an individual key.

\subsubsection{OPTIKS}
OPTIKS focuses on reducing the cost of client-side monitoring, which had remained
a significant overhead in the earlier VKDs: in SEEMless and Parakeet it scales
with the number of a client's key updates and logarithmically with the number of
epochs~\cite[p.~4356]{optiks2024}. Its main construction removes SEEMless's second
(``old'') tree. Instead of proving that a version is absent from an old set,
OPTIKS shows that a key is the latest version in the single remaining tree,
combining a membership proof for the current version with a non-membership proof
for the next, so that in the common case monitoring reduces to verifying one
non-membership proof and storage and update costs are roughly
halved~\cite[p.~4356]{optiks2024}. Around this it adds account decommissioning,
multi-username and multi-device mapping, a bounded-storage ``time periods''
mechanism that lets a deployer trade security for storage, and a crash-tolerant
split read/write architecture~\cite[pp.~4357--4358]{optiks2024}. Its introduction
also offers a useful taxonomy of deployed designs---WhatsApp from SEEMless and
Parakeet, iMessage from CONIKS, and Proton Mail combining all
three~\cite[p.~4355]{optiks2024}. The directory logs identity keys together with
device mappings. Its limitations mirror the line's: soundness still requires the
key's owner to run history checks, and under compaction a user offline during a
deleted period obtains no guarantee for that
interval~\cite[pp.~4357, 4360]{optiks2024}. OPTIKS is thus the strongest
scalability-and-monitoring baseline among the academic systems, yet it still
provides no in-band signal by which one user could warn another.

\subsubsection{PRISM}
PRISM takes the most different route and is best read as a
foil~\cite{pusch2025prism}. Its construction is a \emph{zero-knowledge rollup}: a
zero-knowledge virtual machine (zkVM) operating over a Jellyfish Merkle tree
produces a succinct, constant-size proof of each epoch's state transition and
posts it to a third-party data-availability layer (the Celestia blockchain), so
that every user can verify the integrity of the entire directory state through
that succinct proof rather than by monitoring their own
entry~\cite[\S4]{pusch2025prism}. The proof attests the \emph{semantic}
correctness of each update (that a key change is signed by one of the account's
currently valid, non-revoked private keys) and not merely its structural
well-formedness~\cite[p.~12]{pusch2025prism}. PRISM's sharpest contribution to
this review is diagnostic: it observes that every existing KT system assumes
\emph{someone else will check}, whether the user, gossiping clients, a
blockchain, or third-party auditors~\cite[p.~6]{pusch2025prism}. Its limitations
are that it is presented as an overview rather than a full specification, reports
only a single prover-cost experiment with no proof-size, bandwidth, or
comparative benchmarks, and introduces substantial new trust dependencies in the
data-availability layer, the zkVM toolchain, and a single
sequencer/prover~\cite[pp.~10--11]{pusch2025prism}. In removing per-user
monitoring entirely, PRISM sits at the opposite end of the consistency-model
spectrum from the per-contact, per-lookup designs that the deployed systems
favour.

\subsubsection{Merkle\texorpdfstring{$^2$}{2}}
Merkle\textsuperscript{2} (Hu et al.) is included here not because it is a
key-transparency system, but because it explores an alternative transparency-log
design that influenced later authenticated-directory research. It occupies a
different corner of the design space: a general \emph{transparency-log}
construction aimed at low latency rather than a privacy-oriented key directory.
Its motivation is that earlier transparency logs force a trade-off between
monitoring cost and update latency---to keep monitoring cheap they make users
wait an hour or more for an update to take effect~\cite[p.~1]{hu2021merkle2}.
Merkle\textsuperscript{2} resolves this with a multi-dimensional authenticated
data structure that \emph{nests two kinds of Merkle tree}: a chronological tree,
whose leaves are ordered by append time as in Certificate Transparency, in which
each node carries a prefix tree, whose leaves are ordered lexicographically as in
CONIKS, the nesting that gives the system its name~\cite[p.~1]{hu2021merkle2}.
The two are complementary: a chronological tree frees data owners from checking
every digest but cannot support efficient lookup or revocation, whereas a prefix
tree supports efficient lookup but requires per-digest monitoring. Nesting them
yields both, so that an append costs $O(\log^2 n)$ while a data owner downloads
only polylogarithmic data to monitor across the system's whole
lifetime~\cite[pp.~2, 6]{hu2021merkle2}. Its emphasis, however, is throughput and
latency rather than the directory privacy that messaging demands: as ELEKTRA
notes, Merkle\textsuperscript{2} does not aim for strong key privacy and so leaks
update patterns~\cite[p.~14]{len2023elektra}, and Parakeet faults its assumption
that clients can maintain long-term cryptographic signing keys as
unrealistic~\cite{malvai2023parakeet}. It is therefore best read not as a competitor to
the privacy-preserving VKDs but as evidence that their shared transparency-log
substrate can be optimized for very different objectives once user privacy is not
a first-order goal.

\medskip\noindent
Despite substantial differences in implementation, these constructions converge
on a common architecture (VRF-derived labels, an authenticated directory
supporting membership and non-membership proofs, an append-only history, and
verifiable auditing) and, viewed together, they expose four recurring tensions.
First, \emph{privacy trades against proof size}: hiding more about the directory
generally increases storage and proof complexity. Second, \emph{monitoring cost
trades against infrastructure}: pushing the cost of detection off the client
tends to introduce additional trust assumptions, whether public auditors, a
witness quorum, or a data-availability layer. Third, \emph{multi-device support
trades against state complexity}: richer account models complicate both privacy
and history verification. Fourth, and most consequential for this thesis,
\emph{detection remains external to the conversation}: almost every system relies
on the user, an auditor, or a witness set to notice a malicious directory, and
none provides an in-band channel by which one participant could warn the other
that a substitution has occurred. These tensions persist in the deployed systems
surveyed next, and the last of them is precisely the gap the contributions of
this thesis address.

\subsection{Deployed VKDs in production systems}\label{ssec:bg-vkd-deployed}

The constructions surveyed above have shaped several deployed key-transparency
systems, and examining those deployments matters for two reasons: they show which
design choices have proven practical at scale, and they reveal which limitations
remained after deployment, the operational and user-facing constraints against
which this work is ultimately judged. The deployed ecosystem is dominated by
large messaging providers. We treat the three best-documented systems (WhatsApp,
Signal, and Proton Mail) in turn, and then note Google, Apple, and Keybase more
briefly.

\subsubsection{WhatsApp Key Transparency (AKD)}
WhatsApp operates one of the largest deployed VKDs: an Auditable Key Directory
(AKD), an open-source Rust implementation inspired by SEEMless with extensions
adapted from Parakeet~\cite[p.~4]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. It hashes with Blake3,
publishes an epoch every five minutes, derives leaf positions from a VRF over the
phone number, key version, and a latest-key flag, tombstones removed devices in
production, and signs each directory root with a key whose public half ships
inside the WhatsApp client binary, a deployed signed tree head that hardens
against split views~\cite[pp.~4, 7, 8, 12]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. Audit proofs
are published on Amazon S3 under a write-once retention policy of five years,
allowing independent parties to audit the directory
state~\cite[p.~11]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}, and the directory logs identity keys
only. Two aspects of the deployment are particularly relevant to the problem this
thesis considers. First, client self-monitoring is not enabled---``key history
requests are not yet enabled in the current version of WhatsApp's
clients''~\cite[p.~5]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}---so detection rests on
infrastructure auditing and the peer-and-self lookups performed when a chat is
opened, not on continuous monitoring. Second, when a lookup fails the client
falls back to the manual ceremony rather than an automatic alarm: the session
``should be verified manually with the contact in
question''~\cite[p.~10]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}, and the whitepaper frames key
transparency as a supplement to, not a replacement for, the manual fingerprint
check~\cite[p.~13]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. (The whitepaper's claims are kept
separate from the Meta engineering blog~\cite{lawlor2023whatsapp}, the actual
source of the throughput and third-party-auditor figures.)

\subsubsection{Signal Key Transparency}
Signal's deployment is notable because it closely tracks the emerging IETF Key
Transparency standard: its \texttt{key-transparency-server} implements the draft
with some modifications, exposing Search and Monitor endpoints, public audit
endpoints, and a third-party auditor with test vectors, and it keys the directory
with an elliptic-curve VRF (ECVRF) from the suite family the standard
recommends~\cite{signal-kt-server}. It stores three lookup keys: an identity key
indexed by the account's identifier (the ACI, the per-account identity Signal
assigns independently of the phone number), the phone number itself, and a
username hash. Its wire format pads every prefix proof to the full 256-sibling
copath with stand-in hashes, giving a fixed-size 8{,}192-byte proof regardless of
directory occupancy. As with the other deployed systems reviewed here, detection
ultimately relies on self-monitoring rather than any peer-warning mechanism.
Tracking the IETF draft as it does, Signal's deployment also serves as a useful
indicator of the direction in which standardized key-transparency deployments are
heading.

\subsubsection{Proton Mail Key Transparency}
Proton Mail's ProtonKT is notable for being both deployed and formally analysed,
its design documented in G\"obel's MSc thesis~\cite{goebel2023proton} and a
Transparency-Summit presentation~\cite{proton2023ktdeck}. It is a VKD whose labels
are email addresses and whose values are \emph{Signed Key Lists} (SKLs):
collections of public-key fingerprints signed by a user's primary key, with the
keys themselves stored outside the directory and obsolescence tokens representing
disabled addresses~\cite[pp.~33, 35]{goebel2023proton}. Its most distinctive
feature is its consistency mechanism. Rather than relying on a blockchain, gossip
protocol, or witness quorum, ProtonKT reuses Certificate Transparency (CT),
embedding each epoch's chain hash into a CT-logged TLS certificate, so that
consistency is inherited from the CT ecosystem under a 72-hour maximum merge
delay, with epochs typically published every four
hours~\cite[pp.~37, 45, 46]{goebel2023proton}. Detection is divided among three
actors: users perform Self Audits of their own labels, Promise Audits check that
published commitments are incorporated within the CT merge delay, and External
Audits provide independent oversight~\cite[pp.~50, 52, 53]{goebel2023proton}.
Beyond documenting the deployed system, G\"obel contributes the first
step-by-step specification of a KT scheme and proposes a
\emph{Query-to-SelfAudit Consistency} property, although the accompanying Tamarin
analysis remains partial~\cite[pp.~18, 68, 83]{goebel2023proton}. For the purposes
of this review, the most relevant limitation is ProtonKT's detection model. It
targets email rather than asynchronous messaging, under a different threat
model~\cite[p.~16]{goebel2023proton}, and like the academic constructions it
notifies only the key's owner: a change to Alice's keys is reported to Alice but
never to Bob, and clients and auditors never exchange
information~\cite[pp.~8, 47]{goebel2023proton}. Security even collapses for labels
that no one self-audits, which G\"obel flags as
unsolved~\cite[pp.~76, 78]{goebel2023proton}. G\"obel also identifies the handling
of KT warnings as an open usability problem: on failure Proton's interface shows
the expected fingerprint and prompts a manual comparison, but presents only a
generic verification failure without the exportable technical evidence of the
cause that a user would need to investigate further~\cite[p.~88]{goebel2023proton}. Though not framed as a
peer-warning mechanism, this points to the broader difficulty of communicating KT
failures to end users.

\subsubsection{Google KT, Apple CKV, and Keybase}
Three further deployments round out the picture, less central to the argument but
worth noting. Google Key Transparency is a CONIKS-derived VKD on Trillian that
snapshots a CONIKS-style map into a CT-style log and records, per account,
\emph{who} changed each record, an early precursor of ELEKTRA's device
cross-signing, but it is a general key-discovery service, self-monitoring, and
has been archived since October 2024, making it a design precedent rather than a
live deployment~\cite{google-kt}. Apple Contact Key Verification (iMessage) is a
CONIKS-style design that keeps verification state
device-local~\cite[p.~4355]{optiks2024}\cite{apple-ckv}. Keybase, in production
since 2014, is the deployed multi-device-keychain ancestor, but it targets no
user key privacy, the privacy machinery is precisely what ELEKTRA later
added~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra}.

Despite substantial differences in architecture, consistency mechanisms, and
deployment environments, the deployed systems converge on a common operational
model. Detection rests on self-monitoring, auditor verification, or consistency
checking. None provides a general in-band mechanism through which one user can
automatically warn another about a suspected key substitution. This is the gap
that the remainder of the chapter takes up.

\section{The ACKA (Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement) Framework}\label{sec:bg-acka}

In this section we introduce the ACKA framework of Dowling and
Hale~\cite{dowling2023acka}, the culmination of a line of work arguing that
authentication must itself be ratcheted alongside the key
agreement~\cite{dowling2020nocompromise}, and make precise how this work bridges it to key
transparency. This matters because ACKA is not another verifiable key directory
to be slotted into the comparison of \cref{sec:bg-vkd}: it works in a
\emph{different model}, against a \emph{different adversary}, and it contributes
the continuous-authentication component that a complete solution needs while
leaving abstract exactly the component, a secure log, that key transparency
supplies. We first define its primitives, taking care to define a continuous key
agreement before using it; then we set out its adversary model and how it differs
from the malicious-server adversary that the verifiable key directories of
\cref{sec:bg-vkd} are built to resist, and then we state the bridge, which turns
on a question ACKA itself poses.

Before ACKA can be stated, the object it authenticates must be named. A
\emph{continuous key agreement} (CKA) is, informally, the part of a ratcheting
protocol in which the two parties keep exchanging fresh public key-shares and,
after each exchange, derive a new shared symmetric key; the Double Ratchet of
\cref{ssec:bg-ratchet} is the canonical example. Formally, it is a multi-stage
key-exchange protocol whose participants continually exchange public key-shares
and, after each message, output a fresh symmetric key~\cite[p.~9]{dowling2023acka}.

ACKA (Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement) is composed of three
primitives~\cite[p.~7]{dowling2023acka}. First, the \emph{CKA} is the
key-exchange protocol just defined. Second, \emph{Continuous Entity
Authentication} (CEA), with its security notion CEAsec, is, informally, a
per-epoch authenticator: each epoch it derives a label and a fingerprint from a
chained secret state and the new public key-share, so that the two parties can
check, epoch by epoch, that they are talking to each other and not to an injected
key~\cite[pp.~13--14, 18]{dowling2023acka}. Finally, the \emph{secure logging
scheme} (Logsec) is an append-only, immutable log to which fingerprints are
posted and from which they are queried~\cite[p.~10]{dowling2023acka}. The
detection mechanism is then simple to state: an adversary who injects a forged
key-share must either collide on the honest party's label (producing two
fingerprints at one label, which alerts all communicating parties) or post
under a label the impersonated party never generates, which that party detects on
lookup~\cite[p.~7]{dowling2023acka} (\cref{fig:bg-acka} summarises the
composition).

A point of precision concerns how the framework treats Logsec. It is left
\emph{deliberately abstract}: ``in practice, this may be realized by blockchain
or other distributed ledger protocols; to provide maximum flexibility in choice,
we define the logging scheme here generically''~\cite[p.~10]{dowling2023acka}.
This genericity is a framework design choice rather than a flaw: ACKA fixes what
the secure log must guarantee, append-only immutability, without committing
to how those guarantees are realised, leaving the construction of Logsec
deliberately open.

The adversary ACKA defends against is different from, and along its own axis
stronger than, the malicious-server adversary of the VKD line, and that
difference is the crux of the bridge. ACKA's adversary is a full-compromise
active man-in-the-middle on the \emph{channel}: it may learn the pre-shared
secret state, control the sender's randomness, and actively inject its own forged
ratchet key-share~\cite[pp.~19--20]{dowling2023acka}, and---per Remark~4---may even
hold all long-term authentication secrets~\cite[p.~23]{dowling2023acka}, so that
the construction covers not only full compromise but an unrestricted
adversary~\cite[p.~5]{dowling2023acka}.
But ACKA grants that adversary no power over the \emph{log}: detection holds only
under the assumptions of CEAsec security and an immutable
log~\cite[p.~7]{dowling2023acka}. The malicious, equivocating server that the VKD
line defends against (one that can substitute keys, censor writes, and serve
divergent views) therefore lies \emph{outside} ACKA's model, which trusts its
log to be honest and append-only. The two models are complementary rather than
competing: one adversary controls the wire, the other controls the database, and
no single framework covers both. The construction also bootstraps from a
pre-shared state that must be shared and globally unique across honest pairwise
sessions, though not secret --- which the authors equate with a correctly assumed
trust-on-first-use scenario~\cite[p.~6]{dowling2023acka}, a caveat any
construction built on ACKA inherits. CEAsec itself reduces to the
collision-resistance of the hash function~\cite[p.~26]{dowling2023acka}, precisely
because the adversary is assumed to hold all secret keys, so that collision
resistance must extend to the keying material itself~\cite[p.~23]{dowling2023acka}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption[The three ACKA primitives composed as a pipeline]{The three primitives
of ACKA composed as a pipeline. A continuous key agreement (CKA) supplies each

The theoretical gap this framework leaves open is explicitly acknowledged by ACKA
itself. Asking whether an active man-in-the-middle who has compromised all
secrets can be detected, the authors observe that if the adversary ``completely
controls the channel between the communicating parties \dots\ then the answer is
clearly negative. But what if there exists another channel between the two parties
that the adversary cannot fully control?''~\cite[p.~2]{dowling2023acka}.

ACKA leaves that secondary channel abstract, its honest, immutable Logsec.
Assuming the existence of a perfectly honest, immutable log is, however, a luxury
that practical messaging deployments do not have: as established in
\cref{sec:bg-vkd}, the servers distributing keys are themselves targets of
compromise or coercion. This leaves a fundamental structural gap in the
literature. ACKA requires a channel it can implicitly trust, whereas practical
messaging requires an infrastructure that need not be trusted. ACKA's own
construction further carries three operational limitations that constrain its use
in asynchronous messaging: detection is one-sided---it is the \emph{receiver}
that detects a forgery, and there is no out-of-band channel to warn the
peer~\cite[p.~21]{dowling2023acka}; verification must be performed sequentially
every epoch, with no mechanism for deferred or windowed catch-up; and the
consistency machinery, though defined, is left unused for third-party auditing.
This last point is our own observation rather than a claim of the paper's: ACKA's
detection argument relies only on the log's append-only (exclude) property, and
the paper itself never discusses third-party auditing.

Recognising that ACKA's idealised log and the malicious, equivocating server that
key transparency is built to expose are two halves of a single end-to-end
security picture is what sets the stage for the synthesis this thesis proposes.

The survey establishes five findings.

\paragraph{Finding 1 (Consistency mechanisms).} There is no convergence on a
consistency mechanism: the field spans client
gossip~\cite[p.~7]{melara2015coniks}, CT-anchored map roots (Google), a
$3f{+}1$ witness quorum (Parakeet)~\cite[p.~11]{malvai2023parakeet},
CT-certificate piggybacking (Proton)~\cite[p.~45]{goebel2023proton}, and
zero-knowledge whole-directory verification (PRISM)~\cite[p.~11]{pusch2025prism}.

\paragraph{Finding 2 (Monitoring cost).} Client monitoring cost has fallen
steadily: from CONIKS's per-epoch self-check~\cite[p.~6]{melara2015coniks}, to
SEEMless and Parakeet charging per user update rather than per
epoch~\cite[p.~2]{chase2019seemless}, to OPTIKS's single non-membership proof in
the common case~\cite[p.~4356]{optiks2024}.

\paragraph{Finding 3 (Key coverage).} VKDs differ not only in their consistency
mechanisms but in the cryptographic material they authenticate: identity keys
only (CONIKS, SEEMless, Signal), multi-device key-chains (ELEKTRA,
Keybase)~\cite[p.~1]{len2023elektra}, and email key lists
(Proton)~\cite[p.~33]{goebel2023proton}; \cref{ch:design} extends this comparison
with per-session authenticated labels.

\paragraph{Finding 4 (Proof construction).} Proof construction trades compactness
against structural privacy: Signal deliberately transmits fixed-length
8{,}192-byte proofs that hide the directory's occupancy, whereas leaner
constructions such as CONIKS transmit only the authentication path actually
required, about 1{,}216 bytes~\cite[p.~10]{melara2015coniks}, yielding
smaller proofs at the cost of revealing limited structural information. SEEMless's
measured ${\approx}8{,}600$ bytes~\cite[p.~12]{chase2019seemless} shows that the
heavy end persists even in the academic line, and \cref{ch:eval} places the
proposed construction within this design space.

\paragraph{Finding 5 (Detection).} Despite all these differences, every system
ultimately relies on self-monitoring or external auditing, and none provides a
general in-band peer-warning channel. The closest prior art is ELEKTRA's
reset-only warning~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra} and PRISM's passing suggestion that
commitments could be embedded ``in-band within encrypted
messages''~\cite[p.~5]{pusch2025prism}; PRISM states the diagnosis plainly ---
``all existing KT systems assume that someone else will
check''~\cite[p.~6]{pusch2025prism} --- while the largest deployed system ships
with self-monitoring disabled and a manual fallback on
failure~\cite[pp.~5, 10]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. This recurring design choice
motivates the problem formulated in \cref{sec:bg-open}. The question, therefore,
is not whether existing VKDs can detect directory equivocation or malicious key
substitution, they can, but how that evidence reaches the affected
communicating parties in time to influence the security of a conversation.

\section{Open Problems for Key Transparency in Asynchronous Messaging}\label{sec:bg-open}

The comparison of \cref{sec:bg-compare} and the ACKA limitations of
\cref{sec:bg-acka} leave three problems open for the asynchronous-messaging
setting. We state each precisely and ground it in the systems that exhibit it,
since a problem stated precisely is one whose proposed solution
(\cref{sec:bg-contrib}) can be evaluated rather than merely claimed.

\paragraph{Problem 1 (No in-band peer warning).} Detection in every system
surveyed in \cref{sec:bg-compare} is strictly self-monitoring: only the victim,
checking the entry under their own name, can notice a substituted key, and a peer
who could detect the substitution has no in-band channel by which to warn them.
Concretely, a malicious server shows Bob a key for Alice that Bob's own monitor
never examines (Bob monitors his own entry, not Alice's) while Alice, whose
entry is untouched from her own vantage point, has nothing to report through any
channel Bob reads. The closest prior art does not close this: ELEKTRA's
unsuppressible warning fires only on an account reset, not on a key
substitution~\cite[p.~2]{len2023elektra}, and WhatsApp's deployed client does not
even enable self-monitoring, falling back to the manual ceremony on a failed
check~\cite[pp.~5, 10]{whatsapp2023whitepaper}. No existing VKD provides a
peer-to-peer warning channel, a gap PRISM states in general terms, that ``all
existing KT systems assume that someone else will
check''~\cite[p.~6]{pusch2025prism}.

\paragraph{Problem 2 (Linear offline catch-up).} A mobile,
intermittently-connected client faces an efficiency gap that the per-epoch
protocol does not address: a client offline for $W$ epochs must verify $W$
sequential consistency proofs, each of size $O(\log N)$, for a total of
$O(W \cdot \log N)$. To show this is not hypothetical, consider a directory of $N = 10^7$
users, each maintaining about $c = 5$ active conversations, with pre-keys rotating
every $r = 30$ days. With one label written per conversation, the directory holds
roughly 50 million labels and grows by about 1.67 million records per day, or
about 69{,}000 per hourly epoch. A client offline for a full rotation period faces
$W = r \cdot 24 = 720$ epochs, and $O(W \cdot \log N)$ then amounts to on the order of
17{,}000 sibling hashes for a single month offline, prohibitive on a metered
mobile link. This is an acknowledged open problem rather than an oversight: the
IETF draft flags ``reasonable monitoring windows'' for mobile clients as
unspecified~\cite{ietf-keytrans-protocol}, and CONIKS names the linear offline
cost as an open extension, conceding that its own remedy ``may not be realistic in
all deployments''~\cite[p.~11]{melara2015coniks}.

\paragraph{Problem 3 (Split-view equivocation).} Append-only is not the same as
globally consistent. As \cref{ssec:bg-ct2kt} noted, a malicious server can
maintain two internally consistent logs, presenting tree head $h_A$ to Alice and a
different $h_B$ to Bob, each backed by valid consistency proofs, and serve
each party a different key for the other, with neither able to detect it from
local checks alone. This is the split-view attack, known since Certificate
Transparency. Defeating it requires cross-checking tree heads observed from
different vantage points, and \cref{sec:bg-compare} showed the field has no single
answer: client gossip, CT-anchoring, a witness quorum, and zero-knowledge
whole-directory verification are all in use. What unites them is the recognition
that no purely local check can catch a server maintaining two divergent but
internally valid histories. Detecting equivocation at all requires an independent
mechanism that observes the directory from outside the victim's own session.

These three problems (an absent peer-warning channel, a linear offline catch-up
cost, and a split-view that local checks cannot expose) are the gaps the
contributions of \cref{sec:bg-contrib} set out to close.

\section{Research Gap and Contributions}\label{sec:bg-contrib}

The open problems of \cref{sec:bg-open} leave key transparency for asynchronous
messaging without an in-band peer-warning channel, without an efficient offline
catch-up, and without local detection of server equivocation; ACKA, conversely,
supplies continuous authentication but only by assuming a log it can trust. The
research gap is therefore the absence of a single system that is both resistant to a
malicious directory server and continuously authenticated across the ratchet. The
three problems are not addressed \emph{together} by any system surveyed in
\cref{sec:bg-compare}, and that joint absence is the gap.

These limitations motivate six contributions. Collectively they strengthen key
transparency for asynchronous messaging by adding automatic peer notification,
efficient offline recovery, transparent equivocation detection, and continuous
authentication over an untrusted directory. The first three sit on the key-transparency
log and address those three problems in turn; the fourth carries authentication into the
continuous ratchet setting that ACKA models; the fifth is the conceptual reconciliation
that frames the design, and the sixth is the working system in which all of them are
realised and measured. They are parallel, not layered: the log mechanisms do not depend
on the ACKA instantiation, which realises one operating mode of the system rather than
its foundation.

\paragraph{Contribution 1 --- Dual-index warning scheme.} This addresses Problem 1 by
providing automatic, bidirectional, in-band detection of a man-in-the-middle. Alice and
Bob derive from their shared secret $S$ not only a lookup label but a shared
\emph{warning} label $\ell_{\mathsf{warn}}$, an initially empty leaf in the same log. If
either party detects a key mismatch, it writes to that leaf. Because the label is derived
from $S$, which the server does not hold, the server can neither pre-position a
value on it nor, having committed to the log, suppress a write to it without an
observable, total denial of service. This is the in-band peer-warning channel the
literature lacks. The closest related work is KTACA (Yadav et al.), which achieves
client-side detection but relies on anonymous
lookups~\cite{yadav2020poster,yadav2022fakekey}, a cost Parakeet explicitly objects
to~\cite[p.~14]{malvai2023parakeet} and which our unlinkable derived labels avoid.

\paragraph{Contribution 2 --- Windowed consistency proofs.} These address Problem 2,
reducing the offline catch-up cost from $O(W \cdot \log N)$ to $O(\log N)$, independent
of the window $W$, by verifying a single consistency proof from the tree head the client
last saw to the current one rather than one proof per missed epoch.

\paragraph{Contribution 3 --- Cross-vantage auditor.} This addresses Problem 3: it
cross-checks tree heads observed from a second vantage point and flags two validly-signed
heads at one epoch as an equivocation, detecting the split-view that local checks cannot.
Contributions~1 through~3 are the transparency-log half of the work.

\paragraph{Contribution 4 --- ACKA instantiation (continuous mode).} This is, to our
knowledge, the first concrete instantiation of ACKA's continuous binding over a transparency log: a
per-ratchet-epoch fingerprint and label chain that authenticates the Double Ratchet epoch
by epoch, instantiating the Logsec that ACKA leaves
abstract~\cite[p.~10]{dowling2023acka}. In the prototype the ratchet is driven
deterministically from the shared secret; the in-protocol libsignal hook is future work
(Chapter~7).

\paragraph{Contribution 5 --- Reconciliation of ACKA and key transparency.} The principal
conceptual contribution of this thesis is the reconciliation of ACKA and key
transparency, which are usually read as separate worlds: ACKA authenticates the
continuous key agreement per epoch but assumes an honest, immutable log, whereas the
academic VKD literature builds a malicious-server-resistant directory but authenticates
only a session or identity binding. They differ both in granularity (per-epoch versus
per-session) and, more fundamentally, in their trust assumption about the log: ACKA
trusts it, key transparency does not. We bridge them by instantiating ACKA's ``other
channel'' with a key-transparency log that need not be trusted, reconciling the two
adversary models in one system, realising both as operating modes, and measuring the
trade between them. To our knowledge, no prior work spans the continuous and the
directory worlds. Throughout, we state the coverage trade honestly: we log identity keys
and per-session labels, not the per-user version chains of SEEMless, Parakeet and OPTIKS
or the multi-device key-chains of ELEKTRA, different coverage, leaner proofs, and
automatic in-band detection. (We note for completeness that ``verifiable key exchange''
also names an unrelated line of work on verifiable \emph{quantum} key
distribution~\cite{li2022vqkd}; that setting concerns generating fresh symmetric keys
over quantum channels rather than auditing a classical public-key directory, and is out
of scope here.)

\paragraph{Contribution 6 --- Implementation and evaluation.} This is the engineering
core of the thesis and a contribution in its own right. The core is a Rust \texttt{kt}
crate whose library modules total roughly \num{3600} lines (the full Rust implementation, with the
server, auditor, and benchmark binaries, is about \num{5600}; see \cref{ch:impl}), implementing the
sparse-Merkle-tree directory, the RFC~9162
append-only log with its inclusion and consistency proofs, the Ed25519 signed tree head,
the ECVRF lookup (RFC~9381)~\cite{rfc9381}, the dual-index warning, the windowed catch-up,
and the cross-vantage auditor. It is integrated into the Signal Desktop client, where the
verification is hooked into the key-upload and key-fetch paths. A standalone networked
directory server and an independent auditor service back a two-client, cross-process
demonstration of substitution and equivocation being caught in band, and the Rust and
TypeScript builds are held to byte-for-byte agreement by shared cross-implementation test
vectors. The prototype remains a proof of concept, single-machine and in-memory, with the
genuine X3DH binding and the live-ratchet hook of \cref{ch:discussion} the remaining steps
to deployment.
