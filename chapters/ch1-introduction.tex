\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

Private messaging is now part of everyday personal, professional, and civic communication.
Signal~\cite{signal-home} is an end-to-end encrypted messaging system, and its cryptographic design
has influenced messaging systems beyond the Signal application~\cite{signal-protocol-impact}. The
cryptographic suite that provides its end-to-end encryption is known as the Signal Protocol and is
introduced in \cref{sec:bg-signal}. Its wide adoption makes Signal an important setting in which to
study improvements to key authentication for asynchronous private messaging.

For journalists, activists, abuse survivors, and others whose safety may depend on confidential
communication, encryption alone is not sufficient. A message must be encrypted under the intended
recipient's authentic key. A silent key substitution defeats that guarantee while leaving the
conversation looking secure. An attacker may then read or alter communications without giving either
participant an immediate indication that the channel has been compromised. Detecting such substitution
is therefore particularly important where a loss of confidentiality could cause serious harm.

That protection depends on authentic public keys. End-to-end encryption keeps an intermediary from
reading message content only when each participant encrypts to the intended recipient's genuine key.
In asynchronous messengers such as Signal, clients obtain that key material from provider-operated
infrastructure and trust that what they receive is genuine. That trust is the weak point. If the
infrastructure substitutes an attacker's key for the recipient's, the protocol may build an encrypted
channel to the attacker rather than the intended recipient. This is called a key-substitution, or
``fake-key,'' attack, and it is invisible to the cryptography itself.

The traditional defence is a manual \emph{safety-number} comparison where the two users read a code to each other out of band and confirm that it matches. 
The comparison is sound when completed, but users rarely complete it without guidance. In one study, only about 14\% of unguided pair--application trials succeeded, and just two of twelve pairs completed the comparison in any of the apps tested~\cite{vaziripour2017}. 
Even a successful comparison is a one-off check that says nothing about whether a key changes later.

A messaging service's public-key directory maps user identities to their public identity keys. \emph{Key transparency} (KT) addresses this limitation by publishing authenticated commitments to this directory through a verifiable, append-only history, so that clients can check their keys against a public, consistent record.
Production systems have started deploying it. An example of a production-grade KT system is Signal's optional Automatic Key Verification~\cite{signal-akv}.

However, for key-substitution detection, these systems still rely on the account holder \emph{monitoring their own directory entry}. While a lookup can confirm that a fetched key matches the log, a key substituted \emph{in the log itself} is noticed only by its owner. 
There is no automatic, in-band channel to warn the communicating \emph{peer} that the fetched key is not the intended party's authentic key. Also, a returning client that monitors epoch by epoch faces an offline cost that grows linearly with the number of missed epochs.

Key verification in an asynchronous messenger should therefore operate automatically, warn both
parties in band, and reject a missing or invalid proof instead of accepting the key silently. A
returning client should also be able to verify missed history without downloading one proof for every
missed epoch, while keeping proof sizes and local verification costs practical.

\section{Aim and Approach}\label{sec:intro-aims}

We propose DUET (\emph{DU}al-index, \emph{E}poch-windowed \emph{T}ransparency), a mechanism for
automatic, in-band detection of key substitution without a separate manual comparison, and without
changing the asymptotic complexity of an individual lookup proof, even though the complete mechanism
performs additional label-bound lookups and writes. It combines the dual-index warning scheme with
windowed consistency verification and is integrated into a Signal Desktop prototype (\cref{ch:impl}).
The windowed consistency mechanism further replaces one proof per missed epoch with a single proof
whose size grows logarithmically in the endpoint log sizes.

DUET does not prevent a malicious provider from denying service. Its goal is narrower: a
substituted key, an invalid or missing proof, or an inconsistent directory history must cause
verification to fail rather than allowing the client to accept the attacker's key silently.
\cref{ch:threat} formalizes this distinction between detectable interference and availability.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This thesis makes four principal contributions:

\paragraph{Conceptual.} The conceptual contribution is a \emph{dual-index warning scheme}. We
derive two participant-specific lookup labels and a shared warning label from the conversation's shared secret.
The warning label identifies one shared slot to which either party can write an authenticated alert. For an honestly established and monitored conversation, a malicious server cannot predict and
pre-position that slot before the label is first used. Before the warning commits, a censored write is
detected through a key mismatch alongside a proof-verified empty slot. After the warning commits, the
transition audit detects any removal as an invalid directory transition. The resulting signal allows
both parties to detect the substitution automatically and in band (\cref{sec:design-dual}).

Separately, the thesis instantiates the abstract append-only-log component of the Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement
(ACKA) framework~\cite{dowling2023acka} as the concrete DUET directory. The thesis evaluates the directory's proof sizes and the local costs of
its cryptographic components, and defines the dual-index scheme's guarantee as detection over a
semi-trusted directory rather than prevention. The ACKA framework it builds on is introduced in
\cref{sec:bg-acka}.

\paragraph{Cryptographic.} The cryptographic contribution applies an RFC~9162~\cite{rfc9162} distant-head
consistency proof to offline monitoring. A returning client re-verifies the log with one proof
instead of retrieving one proof for every missed epoch, and the proof size stays logarithmic in the
endpoint log sizes rather than linear in the epochs missed (\cref{sec:design-windowed},
\cref{lem:windowed-size}). A same-head consistency lemma (\cref{lem:dualindex-consistency}) supports
the dual-index design by showing that Alice's peer lookup and Bob's self-check cannot authenticate
different values under one signed head. \cref{ch:design} establishes these properties and
\cref{ch:eval} evaluates their costs.

\paragraph{Systems.} The systems contribution comprises a standalone Rust reference implementation
(the \texttt{duet} crate, an HTTP directory server, and a cross-vantage auditor) and an embedded
TypeScript verifier integrated into a fork of the real Signal Desktop client (\cref{ch:impl}). The
two layers' shared protocol operations are checked byte-for-byte against cross-language test vectors.

\paragraph{Empirical.} The empirical contribution is a benchmark evaluation (metrics M1--M17) with four principal findings. The
implemented VRF-indexed lookup proof is at rough size parity with CONIKS, with the detection channel
CONIKS lacks obtained through additional same-shape lookups rather than a larger proof. The median
full-client verification time remains below \SI{150}{\micro\second} throughout the measured range, up
to $10^7$ labels, and grows far more slowly than $N$. A month-long offline catch-up costs a few
hundred bytes against the hundreds of kilobytes that per-epoch re-fetching would spend. A STRIDE and
MITRE ATT\&CK security evaluation separates the attack scenarios the implemented system detects from
the residual risks that remain (\cref{ch:eval}).

\section{Thesis Roadmap}\label{sec:intro-roadmap}

\Cref{ch:background} surveys the Signal stack, transparency logs, verifiable key directories, and the
ACKA framework, and identifies the research gap. \Cref{ch:threat} fixes the threat model and security
goals; \cref{ch:design} presents the design and instantiates ACKA; \cref{ch:impl} describes the
two-layer implementation and its Signal Desktop integration; and \cref{ch:eval} evaluates the system
empirically and against the threat model. \Cref{ch:discussion,ch:lsepi,ch:conclusion} then cover the
discussion and future work, the legal, social, ethical, and professional dimensions, and the
conclusion. Eight appendices provide the supporting material referenced throughout, from the formal
definitions and algorithms to the benchmark and security-analysis tables, the traceability matrix, and
a glossary.
