\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

This chapter introduces the public-key authentication problem that motivates the thesis and explains
why it matters for Signal's end-to-end security. It first shows how a substituted public key can
undermine an otherwise secure encrypted channel, then states the research questions and
contributions developed in the remainder of the thesis.

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro-motivation}

End-to-end encryption protects a message in transit, but its guarantee is only as strong as the public keys the two parties believe they are using. In asynchronous messaging systems such as Signal, clients normally obtain this key material from provider-operated infrastructure, and trust that what they receive is genuine. That trust is a weak point. If that infrastructure substitutes an attacker's key for the recipient's, the protocol may establish an encrypted channel to the attacker rather than to the intended recipient. This is a key-substitution, or ``fake-key,'' attack, and it is invisible to the cryptography itself.

The traditional defence is a manual \emph{safety-number} comparison where the two users read a code to each other out of band and confirm that it matches. In principle it works. In practice, however, it fails, since it depends on a manual ceremony that people rarely perform. In one study, only about 14\% of attempts at the ceremony succeeded, and just two of twelve pairs completed the comparison in any of the apps tested~\cite{vaziripour2017}; even a successful comparison is a one-off check that says nothing about whether a key changes later. \emph{Key
transparency} (publishing the directory as a verifiable, append-only log so that clients can check their keys against a public, consistent record) is the response the field has converged on, and production systems, such as Signal's Automatic Key Verification, have started deploying it~\cite{signal-akv}.

However, deployed key transparency architectures still leave two critical gaps. \emph{First}, detection is reduced entirely to \emph{self-monitoring}. While a lookup can confirm that a fetched key matches the log, a key substituted \emph{in the log itself} is noticed only by its owner; there is no automatic, in-band channel to warn the communicating \emph{peer} that the key they are about to trust is compromised. \emph{Second}, returning clients face an offline monitoring cost that grows linearly with the number of missed epochs.

This thesis directly addresses both limitations. It asks whether key substitution can be made \emph{automatically} detectable, \emph{in band}, and at low measured cost, so that the \textbf{messaging client verifies the keys in the background}, rather than relying on users to perform manual ceremonies. The scheme it develops in answer, DUET (\emph{DU}al-index, \emph{E}poch-windowed \emph{T}ransparency), applies to any messenger with a semi-trusted key directory and is demonstrated through an integration into the Signal Desktop client.

DUET does not prevent a malicious provider from denying service. Its goal is narrower: a substituted
key, an invalid or missing proof, or an inconsistent directory history must cause verification to
fail rather than allowing the client to accept the attacker's key silently. \Cref{ch:threat}
formalizes this distinction between detectable interference and availability.

\section{Aims and Objectives}\label{sec:intro-aims}

This section defines what the thesis sets out to achieve and how its success is judged. It states the
central claim the dissertation defends, then decomposes that claim into four research questions, each
paired later with the evidence that answers it and the criterion by which it could have been
falsified.

\paragraph{Thesis Statement.} The central claim this dissertation defends is that automatic, in-band
detection of key substitution can be added to a messaging key-directory architecture at essentially the
asymptotic cost the directory already pays, without a manual user ceremony. It does so without
enlarging the lookup proof, and while replacing one proof per missed epoch with a single proof whose
size grows logarithmically in the endpoint log sizes.

\paragraph{Research Questions.} The work is organized around four research questions, each answered by
a specific later chapter:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (detection).] For an honestly established, subsequently monitored conversation, can key
  substitution over a semi-trusted directory be detected automatically and in band, by both the
  victim and the peer, without an out-of-band user ceremony? \emph{(Answered by the design of \cref{ch:design} and the security evaluation of
  \cref{sec:eval-security}.)}
  \item[RQ2 (cost).] What does such detection cost in lookup-proof size and client verification time,
  and how does it compare with existing key-transparency systems? \emph{(Answered by
  \cref{sec:eval-micro,sec:eval-compare}.)}
  \item[RQ3 (offline).] Can a client that has been offline for an arbitrarily long window
  re-synchronize with the log in a single proof, at a cost that grows logarithmically in the
  endpoint log sizes rather than linearly in the epochs missed? \emph{(Answered by the
  windowed consistency proof of \cref{sec:design-windowed} and its measurement in
  \cref{ssec:eval-catchup}.)}
  \item[RQ4 (client-integration feasibility).] Does the scheme integrate into a real messaging-client
  codebase (Signal Desktop) while retaining practical, measured component costs, rather than only into a standalone model? \emph{(Answered by the Signal Desktop
  integration of \cref{ch:impl} and the deployment analysis of \cref{sec:eval-deploy}.)}
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This thesis makes four principal contributions:

\paragraph{Conceptual.} The conceptual contribution is a \emph{dual-index warning scheme}. In it, the
HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function (HKDF) turns a conversation's shared secret
into two directional lookup labels and one shared warning slot to which either party can write an
authenticated alert. For an honestly established and monitored conversation, a malicious server cannot predict and
pre-position that slot before the label is first used, and cannot suppress a committed alert
undetectably afterwards, so a silent substitution becomes an automatic, bidirectional, in-band
detection (\cref{sec:design-dual}). More broadly,
the thesis presents this scheme as a concrete instantiation and measurement of the abstract
append-only-log component of the Authenticated Continuous Key Agreement (ACKA)
framework~\cite{dowling2023acka} (the instantiation claimed, with its scope, in
\cref{sec:bg-contrib}), and defines its guarantee
precisely as detection over a semi-trusted directory rather than prevention.

\paragraph{Cryptographic.} The cryptographic contribution applies an RFC~9162 distant-head
consistency proof to offline monitoring. A returning client re-verifies the log with one proof
instead of retrieving one proof for every missed epoch, and the proof size stays logarithmic in the
endpoint log sizes rather than linear in the epochs missed (\cref{sec:design-windowed},
\cref{lem:windowed-size}). A same-head consistency lemma (\cref{lem:dualindex-consistency}) supports
the dual-index design by showing that Alice's peer lookup and Bob's self-check cannot authenticate
different values under one signed head. \cref{ch:design} establishes these properties and
\cref{ch:eval} evaluates their costs.

\paragraph{Systems.} The systems contribution comprises a standalone Rust reference implementation
(the \texttt{duet} crate, an HTTP directory server, and a cross-vantage auditor) and an embedded
TypeScript verifier integrated into a fork of the real Signal Desktop client (\cref{ch:impl}). The
two layers' shared protocol operations are checked byte-for-byte against cross-language test vectors.

\paragraph{Empirical.} The empirical contribution is a benchmark campaign (metrics M1--M13) with four principal findings. The
implemented VRF-indexed lookup proof is at rough size parity with CONIKS, with the detection channel
CONIKS lacks obtained through additional same-shape lookups rather than a larger proof. The median
full-client verification time remains below \SI{150}{\micro\second} throughout the measured range, up
to $10^7$ labels, and grows far more slowly than $N$. A month-long offline catch-up costs a few
hundred bytes against the hundreds of kilobytes that per-epoch re-fetching would spend. A systematic security
evaluation using the STRIDE threat-classification framework and the MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge base
covers the resulting system (\cref{ch:eval}).

\section{Thesis Roadmap}\label{sec:intro-roadmap}

\Cref{ch:background} surveys the Signal protocol stack, transparency logs, verifiable key directories,
and the ACKA framework, and identifies the research gap. \Cref{ch:threat} fixes the threat model,
attack taxonomy, and security goals. \Cref{ch:design} presents the design (the combined-tree
architecture, the dual-index warning scheme, and windowed consistency proofs) and instantiates
ACKA. \Cref{ch:impl} describes the two-layer implementation and its integration into Signal Desktop.
\Cref{ch:eval} evaluates the system empirically and against the threat model, and draws out the
implications for deployment. \Cref{ch:discussion} discusses the results, limitations, and future work.
\Cref{ch:lsepi} considers the legal, social, ethical, and professional dimensions.
\Cref{ch:conclusion} concludes the thesis. Supporting material is provided in the appendices: formal
definitions (\cref{app:definitions}), algorithms (\cref{app:algorithms}), the implementation and
API reference (\cref{app:apiref}), the test catalogue (\cref{app:tests}), the full benchmark
results (\cref{app:benchmarks}), the security-analysis tables (\cref{app:security}), a traceability
matrix linking each claim to its mechanism, test, and evidence (\cref{app:traceability}), and a
glossary of terms (\cref{app:glossary}).
