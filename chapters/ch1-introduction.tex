\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro-motivation}

End-to-end encryption protects a message in transit, but its guarantee is only as strong as the
public keys the two parties believe they are using. In every deployed encrypted messenger those keys
are distributed by a server: to start a conversation, a client fetches the recipient's key material
from the provider's directory and trusts that what it received is genuine. That trust is the weak
point. A compromised, coerced, or simply malicious directory can hand a client the attacker's key in
place of the recipient's, and the encryption then protects a channel to the attacker --- a
key-substitution, or ``fake-key,'' attack that is invisible to the cryptography itself.

The traditional defence is the manual \emph{safety-number} comparison: the two users read a code
to each other out of band and confirm it matches. It works in principle but fails in practice,
because it depends on a human ritual that people do not perform. In one study only about 14\% of
users completed the ceremony without guidance~\cite{vaziripour2017}, and even a successful comparison
is a one-off check that says nothing about whether a key changes later. \emph{Key
transparency} (publishing the directory as a verifiable, append-only log so that clients can check
their keys against a public, consistent record) is the response the field has converged on, and it
now ships in production systems, most recently as Signal's optional Automatic Key
Verification~\cite{signal-akv}. But deployed key transparency still leaves the decisive check to
\emph{self-monitoring}: a lookup can confirm that a fetched key matches the log, yet a key
substituted \emph{in the log itself} is noticed only by its owner, and there is no automatic,
in-band channel by which the \emph{peer} on the other side of a conversation is warned that the
key they are about to trust is wrong. The verification burden, in the end, still falls on the
user.

This thesis addresses that gap. It asks whether key substitution can be made \emph{automatically}
detectable, \emph{in band}, and at a cost a real messaging service could afford --- so that the
directory checks the keys on the users' behalf rather than asking the users to check them. The
scheme it develops in answer, DUET (\emph{DU}al-index, \emph{E}poch-windowed \emph{T}ransparency),
applies to any messenger with a semi-trusted key directory and is demonstrated through an
integration into the Signal Desktop client.

\section{Aims and objectives}\label{sec:intro-aims}

\paragraph{Thesis statement.} The central claim this dissertation defends is that automatic, in-band
detection of key substitution can be added to a deployed messaging key directory at essentially the
asymptotic cost the directory already pays --- without a manual user ceremony, without enlarging the
lookup proof, and without imposing an offline-catch-up cost that grows with the time a client spends
away.

\paragraph{Research questions.} The work is organised around four research questions, each answered by
a specific later chapter:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (detection).] Can key substitution over a semi-trusted directory be detected
  automatically and in band, by both the victim and the peer, without an out-of-band user
  ceremony? \emph{(Answered by the design of \cref{ch:design} and the security evaluation of
  \cref{sec:eval-security}.)}
  \item[RQ2 (cost).] What does such detection cost in lookup-proof size and client verification time,
  and how does it compare with existing key-transparency systems? \emph{(Answered by
  \cref{sec:eval-micro,sec:eval-compare}.)}
  \item[RQ3 (offline).] Can a client that has been offline for an arbitrarily long window
  re-synchronise with the log at a cost independent of that window's length? \emph{(Answered by the
  windowed consistency proof of \cref{sec:design-windowed} and its measurement in
  \cref{ssec:eval-catchup}.)}
  \item[RQ4 (deployability).] Does the scheme integrate into a real, deployed messaging client at a
  practical cost, rather than only into a standalone model? \emph{(Answered by the Signal Desktop
  integration of \cref{ch:impl} and the deployment analysis of \cref{sec:eval-deploy}.)}
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The contributions fall into four kinds.

\paragraph{Conceptual.} A \emph{dual-index warning scheme}: a per-conversation construction that gives
each pair of users a shared, HKDF-derived warning slot in the directory that either party can raise
and that a malicious server can neither pre-position nor censor undetectably, turning a silent
substitution into an automatic, bidirectional, in-band alert (\cref{sec:design-dual}). More broadly,
the thesis provides the first concrete instantiation, and measurement, of the abstract
append-only-log component of the ACKA framework~\cite{dowling2023acka}, and frames the guarantee
precisely as detection over a semi-trusted directory rather than prevention.

\paragraph{Cryptographic.} A \emph{windowed consistency proof} that lets an offline client re-verify
the log in a single proof whose size is bounded by the endpoint log sizes and is independent of the
number of missed epochs (\cref{sec:design-windowed}, \cref{lem:windowed-size}), and a formal
detection experiment for the dual-index scheme, reduced to standard assumptions
(\cref{def:dualindex-detect}).

\paragraph{Systems.} A complete implementation in two independent layers held to byte-for-byte
parity: a standalone Rust reference implementation (the \texttt{kt} crate, an HTTP directory server,
and a cross-vantage auditor) and an embedded TypeScript verifier integrated into a fork of the real
Signal Desktop client (\cref{ch:impl}).

\paragraph{Empirical.} A benchmark campaign (metrics M1--M13) establishing that the dual-index lookup
proof is at size parity with CONIKS while adding a detection channel it lacks, that full client
verification is under \SI{150}{\micro\second} and independent of directory size, and that a
month-long offline catch-up costs a few hundred bytes against the hundreds of kilobytes naïve
monitoring would spend; together with a systematic security evaluation (STRIDE and MITRE ATT\&CK
coverage) of the resulting system (\cref{ch:eval}).

\section{Thesis roadmap}\label{sec:intro-roadmap}

\Cref{ch:background} surveys the Signal protocol stack, transparency logs, verifiable key directories,
and the ACKA framework, and identifies the research gap. \Cref{ch:threat} fixes the threat model,
attack taxonomy, and security goals. \Cref{ch:design} presents the design (the combined-tree
architecture, the dual-index warning scheme, and windowed consistency proofs) and instantiates
ACKA. \Cref{ch:impl} describes the two-layer implementation and its integration into Signal Desktop.
\Cref{ch:eval} evaluates the system empirically and against the threat model, and draws out the
implications for deployment. \Cref{ch:discussion} discusses results, limitations, and future work;
\cref{ch:lsepi} covers the legal, social, ethical, and professional dimensions; and
\cref{ch:conclusion} concludes.
